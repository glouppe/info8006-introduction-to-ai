\begin{recap}
    \topic{Search problem}
    A \term{search problem} is defined by the \term{states} $s \in \mathcal{S}$, an \term{initial state} $s_1 \in \mathcal{S}$, the \term{actions} $\text{actions}(s) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ available in a state $s$, a \term{transition model} $s' = \text{result}(s, a)$, the \term{goal states} $G \subseteq \mathcal{S}$ and positive \term{step costs} $c(s, a, s')$. The \term{state space} is the set of states reachable from $s_1$. A \term{solution} is an action sequence $a_{1:T}$ such that $s_{t+1} = \text{result}(s_t, a_t)$ and $s_{T+1} \in G$; an \term{optimal solution} minimizes the \term{path cost} $\sum_{t=1}^T c(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$.

    \topic{Search algorithm}
    \term{Tree search} expands the nodes of the \term{fringe} until a goal state is reached. \term{Graph search} also keeps the set of expanded states, the \term{closed} set, which avoids repeated states and cycles.

    \topic{Search strategies}
    A \term{strategy} is the order in which the fringe is expanded, where $g(n)$ is the path cost from $s_1$ to $n$, $h(n)$ a heuristic and $f(n) = g(n) + h(n)$. Below, $b$ is the maximum branching factor, $d$ the depth of the least-cost solution, $m$ the maximum path length (possibly infinite), $C^*$ the optimal path cost and $\epsilon > 0$ a lower bound on the step costs.

    \begin{center}
        \small
        \begin{tabular}{llcccc}
            \toprule
            Strategy & Fringe & Complete & Optimal & Time & Space \\
            \midrule
            Depth-first (DFS) & LIFO stack & if no cycles & no & $O(b^m)$ & $O(bm)$ \\
            Breadth-first (BFS) & FIFO queue & yes & if $c$ uniform & $O(b^d)$ & $O(b^d)$ \\
            Iterative deepening & DFS, depth $1, 2, \ldots$ & yes & if $c$ uniform & $O(b^d)$ & $O(bd)$ \\
            Uniform-cost (UCS) & priority $g(n)$ & if $c \geq \epsilon$ & yes & $O(b^{C^*/\epsilon})$ & $O(b^{C^*/\epsilon})$ \\
            Greedy & priority $h(n)$ & if no cycles & no & $O(b^m)$ & $O(b^m)$ \\
            \Astar{} & priority $f(n)$ & yes & $h$ admissible (tree) & $O(b^m)$ & $O(b^m)$ \\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
    \end{center}

    \topic{Heuristics}
    A \term{heuristic} $h(n) \geq 0$ estimates the cost of the cheapest path from $n$ to a goal state, with $h(n) = 0$ at a goal state, often as the exact cost of a \term{relaxed problem}. Let $h^*(n)$ be the true cost from $n$ to a nearest goal.
    \begin{itemize}
        \item \term{Admissible}: $h(n) \leq h^*(n)$ for all $n$, \ie{} $h$ never overestimates. Tree-search \Astar{} is then optimal.
        \item \term{Consistent}: $h(n) \leq c(n, a, n') + h(n')$ for every $n$ and every successor $n'$ generated by an action $a$. Then $h$ is admissible, $f$ is non-decreasing along any path and graph-search \Astar{} is optimal.
        \item \term{Dominance}: if $h_1$ and $h_2$ are admissible and $h_2(n) \geq h_1(n)$ for all $n$, then $h_2$ dominates $h_1$ and is better for search. $\max(h_a, h_b)$ is admissible and dominates both.
    \end{itemize}
\end{recap}

\titledquestion{Path finding}

Tram'Ardent maintains the green areas along the Liège tram line with a new robot. This robot's goal is to convey the grass cuttings left by the gardeners to a container as fast as possible. Assuming that
\begin{itemize}
    \item the fuel cost used by the robot is negligible
    \item the robot can only navigate on guides that are placed in a rectangular grid configuration and can locate itself on the grid
    \item there is only one pile and one container by zone (\ie{} by grid) for which positions are known by the robot
    \item the speed of the robot is constant and the grass loading/unloading is instantaneous
    \item the robot can only carry a limited weight of grass at once
    \item the robot can know the initial weight of grass on the pile.
\end{itemize}
For a given grid

\begin{parts}
    \part Formulate a corresponding search problem.

    \begin{solution}
        \textbf{State representation}: Assuming a $H \times W$ grid, the state is represented by the position $(i, j) \in \{1, \ldots, W\} \times \{1, \ldots, H\}$ of the robot, the remaining weight of grass $w_G$ and a flag $L$ indicating whether the robot is loaded or not. Following other assumptions, we also define the location of the pile and the container respectively by $P = (i_p, j_p)$ and $C = (i_c, j_c)$ as well as the weight limit of the robot $w_{\text{max}}$.

        \textbf{Initial state}: No specific information is given, we can assume it starts anywhere on the grid, unloaded. $s_1 = (i_0, j_0, w_{G0}, \mathit{False})$

        \textbf{Actions}: $a \in \{\mathit{LEFT}, \mathit{UP}, \mathit{RIGHT}, \mathit{DOWN}\}$

        \textbf{Transition model}:
        \[
            (i, j)' = \begin{cases*}
                (i + 1, j)\ & \text{if $a = \mathit{RIGHT}$ and $i < W$} \\
                (i, j + 1)\ & \text{if $a = \mathit{UP}$ and $j < H$} \\
                (i - 1, j)\ & \text{if $a = \mathit{LEFT}$ and $i > 1$} \\
                (i, j - 1)\ & \text{if $a = \mathit{DOWN}$ and $j > 1$} \\
                (i, j)\ & \text{otherwise}
            \end{cases*}
        \]
        \[
            w_G' = \begin{cases*}
                w_G - \min(w_G, w_{\text{max}})\ & \text{if $\lnot L$ and $(i, j)' = P$} \\
                w_G\ & \text{otherwise}
            \end{cases*}
        \]
        \[
            L' = \begin{cases*}
                \text{True} & \text{if $L$ and $(i, j)' \neq C$} \\
                \text{True} & \text{if $w_G > 0$ and $(i, j)' = P$} \\
                \text{False} & \text{otherwise}
            \end{cases*}
        \]

        \textbf{Goal states}: $G = \cbk{s \in \mathcal{S} : \lnot L \text{ and } w_G = 0}$.

        \textbf{Step cost}: Under the given assumptions, every step from one node to another is equivalent. Then $c(s, a, s') = 1$ is relevant. Note that it could be any constant step cost.
    \end{solution}

    \part Knowing that the terrain can be rugged at specific places, causing slow down of the robot, propose an admissible heuristic which estimates the remaining cost at any node.

    \begin{solution}
        As usual in grid problems, the Manhattan or the Euclidean distance is admissible as long as every step costs at least 1\footnote{Choosing a constant step cost $K > 1$ does not break admissibility in this case. If $K \in \,]0, 1[$, then $h(s)$ is not admissible but $K h(s)$ is.}. However, the robot can go back and forth if $w_G > w_{\text{max}}$. Then an admissible heuristic would be
        \[
            h(s) = \begin{cases*}
                0\ & \text{if $\lnot L$ and $w_G = 0$} \\
                |i_c - i| + |j_c - j| + 2\ceil{\frac{w_G}{w_{\text{max}}}}\big(|i_c - i_p| + |j_c - j_p|\big)\ & \text{if $L$} \\
                |i_p - i| + |j_p - j| + \big(2\ceil{\frac{w_G}{w_{\text{max}}}} - 1\big)\big(|i_c - i_p| + |j_c - j_p|\big)\ & \text{if $\lnot L$}
            \end{cases*}
        \]
        which would have been the true remaining cost if the terrain was not rugged.
    \end{solution}

    \part The robot can now also move diagonally, a diagonal step costing $\sqrt{2}$. Is your heuristic still admissible? If not, what should you change? What if the robot is twice as fast diagonally, so that a diagonal step costs $\sqrt{2}/2$?

    \begin{solution}
        The Manhattan distance cannot be used anymore, only the Euclidean distance would be admissible (amongst the previous proposals) or any other function dominated by the Euclidean distance.

        If the robot is twice as fast diagonally, a unit of length costs at least $1/2$, so the Euclidean distance divided by 2 is admissible. The most limiting case is a perfect diagonal going from $P$ to $C$.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Graph search}

For each of the following search algorithms, apply the \textsc{Graph-Search} algorithm of lecture 2, reproduced below, to the undirected graph below and give the order in which the states are expanded, as well as the final path returned by the algorithm. Ties are broken by alphabetical order on the states and, between two nodes of the same state, in favour of the one inserted first in the fringe. The goal test is applied when a node leaves the fringe, so the goal state is returned without ever being expanded.

\begin{pseudocode}
    \kw{function} \proc{Graph-Search}(\pvar{problem}, \pvar{fringe}) \kw{returns} a solution, or failure \\
    \> \pvar{closed} $\gets$ an empty set \\
    \> \pvar{fringe} $\gets$ \proc{Insert}(\proc{Make-Node}(\proc{Initial-State}[\pvar{problem}]), \pvar{fringe}) \\
    \> \kw{loop do} \\
    \> \> \kw{if} \pvar{fringe} is empty \kw{then return} failure \\
    \> \> \pvar{node} $\gets$ \proc{Remove-Front}(\pvar{fringe}) \\
    \> \> \kw{if} \proc{Goal-Test}(\pvar{problem}, \proc{State}[\pvar{node}]) \kw{then return} \pvar{node} \\
    \> \> \kw{if} \proc{State}[\pvar{node}] is not in \pvar{closed} \kw{then} \\
    \> \> \> add \proc{State}[\pvar{node}] to \pvar{closed} \\
    \> \> \> \pvar{fringe} $\gets$ \proc{InsertAll}(\proc{Expand}(\pvar{node}, \pvar{problem}), \pvar{fringe}) \\
    \> \kw{end}
\end{pseudocode}

\begin{parts}
    \part Depth-first search (DFS)

    \begin{solution}
        We use a Last-In First-Out (LIFO) fringe, \ie{} a \emph{stack}, to perform the search. In this stack, we keep track of the states that are reachable from the closed states. These states are annotated with the state they were expanded from, which allows to reconstruct the path at the end. At each step, the state at the \emph{top} of the stack is \emph{popped}. If its state is not closed yet, it is expanded, which consists in \emph{pushing} its successors to the \emph{top} of the stack.

        In the tables below, each row corresponds to one node leaving the fringe, the next one to leave being the rightmost. For readability, a node whose state is already closed is not written down, since it would be discarded when it leaves the fringe; a node whose state gets closed while it waits is discarded in the same way.

        \begin{center}
            \small
            \begin{tabular}{ll}
                \toprule
                Fringe (LIFO stack) & Closed \\
                \midrule
                S & \\
                C(S) B(S) A(S) & S \\
                C(S) B(S) B(A) & S A \\
                C(S) B(S) E(B) D(B) C(B) & S A B \\

            \end{tabular}
        \end{center}

        Expansion: S, A, B, C, D, E. Path: S, A, B, D, E, G.
    \end{solution}

    \part Breadth-first search (BFS)

    \begin{solution}
        We use a First-In First-Out (FIFO) fringe, \ie{} a \emph{queue}, to keep track of the reachable states. At each step, the state at the \emph{front} of the queue is popped. If its state is not closed yet, it is expanded, which consists in pushing its successors to the \emph{back} of the queue.

    \begin{solution}

    \part \Astar{}

    \begin{solution}
        The priority of a node $n$ is the \emph{estimated} cost $f(n) = g(n) + h(n)$ of the cheapest solution through $n$.
    \end{solution}

    \begin{subparts}
        \subpart Is the heuristic admissible? If not, make it admissible.

        \begin{solution}
            A heuristic $h$ is admissible if $0 \leq h(n) \leq h^*(n)$, where $h^*(n)$ is the true forward cost of $n$. In the graph, $h(\text{E}) = 19$ is larger than $h^*(\text{E}) = 11$, making $h$ non-admissible. This costs the optimal path: D is expanded before E and \Astar{} returns S, B, D, G, of cost 21, instead of S, B, E, D, G, of cost 20.

            Setting $h(\text{E}) = 11$ is sufficient to make $h$ admissible.
        \end{solution}

        \subpart Is the heuristic consistent? If not, can we apply \textsc{Graph-Search}?

        \begin{solution}
            A heuristic $h$ is consistent if $h(n) \leq c(n, a, n') + h(n')$ for every node $n$ and every successor $n'$ generated by an action $a$. Because this graph is undirected, consistency requires $c(n, a, n') \geq \abs{h(n) - h(n')}$ for every pair of neighbors, which does not hold here: $h(\text{A}) = 20 > c(\text{A}, a, \text{S}) + h(\text{S}) = 1 + 11$. Correcting $h(\text{E})$ to 11 does not help, as this edge still violates the condition.

            This is problematic as it could create situations where a node has been expanded within a path that is not optimal, and cannot be re-expanded later on within the optimal one. Hence, the algorithm loses its optimality.

            Fortunately, there is a modification of \textsc{Graph-Search} that can handle inconsistent heuristics. If we annotate the nodes in the closed set with the priority at which they were expanded, we can re-expand a node if it has a better (lower) priority than before. The priority in the closed set is then updated.
        \end{solution}

        \subpart Apply \Astar{} with the admissible heuristic, using the variant of \textsc{Graph-Search} that re-expands a closed state reached with a lower priority.

        \begin{solution}
            For readability, nodes that are already in the closed set with a lower priority are not added to the fringe. The re-expansion is shown in red.

                    A(S, 21) B(S, 16) C(S, 13) & S(11) \\
                    A(S, 21) B(C, 17) B(S, 16) & S(11) C(13) \\
                    A(B, 29) A(S, 21) E(B, 20) B(C, 17) D(B, 14) & S(11) C(13) B(16) \\

\titledquestion{Maze car \source{CS188, Spring 2014}}

Consider a car agent which has to exit a maze. At all time-steps, the agent points at direction $d \in \cbk{\text{N}, \text{S}, \text{E}, \text{W}}$. With a single action, the agent can either move forward at an adjustable velocity $v \in [0, V]$ or turn. The turning actions are \texttt{left} and \texttt{right}, which change the agent's direction by 90 degrees. Turning is only permitted when the velocity is zero (and leaves it at zero). The moving actions are \texttt{faster} and \texttt{slower}. The action \texttt{faster} increments the velocity by 1 and \texttt{slower} decrements the velocity by 1; in both cases the agent then moves a number of squares equal to its \emph{new} velocity. Any action that would result in a collision with a wall is illegal. Any action that would reduce $v$ below 0 or above a maximum speed $V$ is also illegal. The agent's goal is to find a plan which parks it (stationary) on the exit square using as few actions (time steps) as possible.

\begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[world diagram]
        \draw[grid lines] (0, 0) grid (5, 5);
        \draw[wall] (1, 3) -- (1, 4) -- (2, 4);
        \draw[wall] (1, 3) -- (2, 3) -- (2, 1);
        \draw[wall] (3, 2) -- (3, 4);
        \draw[wall] (4, 2) -- (4, 4);
        \draw[wall] (0, 2) -- (1, 2);
        \draw[wall] (0, 1) -- (1, 1);
        \draw[world border] (0, 0) rectangle (5, 5);
        \pic at (0.5, 4.5) {exit};
        \pic at (0.5, 0.5) {car};
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

As an example, in the maze above, if the agent is initially stationary, it might first turn to the east using \texttt{right}, then move one square east using \texttt{faster}, then two more squares east using \texttt{faster} again. However, the agent will have to slow to take the turn.

\begin{parts}
    \part For a grid of size $M \times N$, what is the size of the state space? Assume that all configurations are reachable from the starting state.

    \begin{solution}
        $N \times M \times 4 \times (V + 1)$
    \end{solution}

    \part What is the maximum branching factor of this problem? Assume that illegal actions are simply not returned by the successor function.

    \begin{solution}
        The maximum branching factor is 3, and this happens when the agent is stationary. While stationary, it can take the following 3 actions: \texttt{faster}, \texttt{left}, \texttt{right}.
    \end{solution}

    \part Is the Manhattan distance from the agent's location to the exit's location admissible?

    \begin{solution}
        No because it does not take the velocity of the agent into account. For instance, in a long straight path, the number of steps could be smaller than the number of squares.
    \end{solution}

    \part If we used an inadmissible heuristic in \Astar{} tree search, could it change the completeness of the search? And the optimality?

    \begin{solution}
        With an inadmissible heuristic, \Astar{} will still find a final state if there exists a reachable one (complete), but it could stop the search before finding the optimal one (non optimal).
    \end{solution}

    \part State and motivate a non-trivial admissible heuristic for this problem.

    \begin{solution}
        Some examples of admissible heuristics are:
        \begin{itemize}
            \item The Manhattan distance to the exit divided by the maximum speed $V$, since the agent covers at most $V$ squares per action.
            \item The current velocity $v$, since the agent needs $v$ actions of \texttt{slower} to park on the exit.
            \item The maximum of the two, $\max\cbk{\frac{\text{Manhattan}}{V}, v}$, which \emph{dominates} both and is still admissible, as both are lower bounds on the true forward cost. Their \emph{sum} is not admissible: two squares away from the exit at velocity $v = 2$, parking takes 2 actions (\texttt{slower} twice), while the sum gives $2/V + 2 > 2$.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{solution}

    \part Give a general advantage that an inadmissible heuristic might have over an admissible one.

    \begin{solution}
        The time to solve an \Astar{} tree search problem is a function of two factors: the number of nodes expanded, and the time spent per node.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item An inadmissible heuristic may be faster to compute, leading to a solution that is obtained faster due to less time spent per node.
            \item Admissible heuristics are sometimes too conservative (well below $h^*$). An inadmissible heuristic could be a better estimate of the true forward cost $h^*$ (although it will overestimate at times), thus expanding fewer nodes.
        \end{itemize}
        We lose the guarantee of optimality by using an inadmissible heuristic. However, sometimes, we may be okay with finding a suboptimal solution faster.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Imperfect heuristic \source{CS188, Spring 2019}}

Consider a graph search problem where all edges have a unit cost and the optimal solution has cost $C^*$. Let $h(n)$ be a heuristic which is $\max \cbk{h^*(n) - k, 0}$, where $h^*(n)$ is the true forward cost of $n$ and $k \leq C^*$ is a non-negative constant.

\begin{parts}
    \part Is $h$ admissible?

    \begin{solution}
        Yes. If $h^* \geq k$, $h^* - k \leq h^*$, otherwise $0 \leq h^*$.
    \end{solution}

    \part Which of the following is the most reasonable description of how much more work will be done, \ie{} how many more nodes will be expanded, with heuristic $h$ compared to $h^*$, as a function of $k$?
    \begin{enumerate}[label=$\bigcirc$]
        \item Constant in $k$
        \item Linear in $k$
        \item \correct{Exponential in $k$}
        \item Unbounded
    \end{enumerate}

    \begin{solution}
        \Astar{} expands a node $n$ when $f(n) = g(n) + h(n) \leq C^*$. With $h(n) = \max\cbk{h^*(n) - k, 0}$, this happens as soon as $g(n) + h^*(n) \leq C^* + k$, \ie{} as soon as the cheapest path through $n$ costs at most $k$ more than the optimal one. Since all edges have a unit cost, such a path is at most $k$ steps longer than the optimal path, and there are $O(b^k)$ of them in the worst case, for a branching factor $b$. The additional work is therefore exponential in $k$.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Pacmen \source{Exam, January 2023}}

Pacman and his friends have decided to combine forces and go on the offensive, and are now chasing ghosts! In a grid of size $M \times N$, Pacman and $P - 1$ of his friends are moving around to collectively eliminate all of the ghosts in the grid by stepping on the same square as each of them. Moving into the same square as a ghost will eliminate it from the grid. At every turn, Pacman and his friends may choose one of the four (\texttt{north}, \texttt{south}, \texttt{east}, \texttt{west}) actions but may not collide with each other. In other words, any action that would result in two or more Pacmen occupying the same square will result in no movement for either of the Pacmen. Additionally, Pacman and his friends are \emph{indistinguishable} from each other. There are a total of $G$ ghosts, which are also indistinguishable from each other and cannot move.

Treating this as a search problem, we consider each configuration of the grid to be a state, and the goal state to be the configuration where all ghosts have been eliminated from the board. The cost of each turn (all Pacmen move once) is $1$. Below is an example starting state, as well as an example of goal state.

\begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[world diagram]
        \draw[grid lines] (0, 0) grid (6, 6);
        \draw[world border] (0, 0) rectangle (6, 6);
        \foreach \x/\y in {0/0, 3/3, 2/5}
            \pic at (\x + 0.5, \y + 0.5) {pacman};
        \foreach \x/\y in {1/1, 0/1, 4/2, 5/5}
            \pic at (\x + 0.5, \y + 0.5) {ghost};
        \node[diagram caption, below] at (3, -0.2) {Initial state example};

        \begin{scope}[shift={(8, 0)}]
            \draw[grid lines] (0, 0) grid (6, 6);
            \draw[world border] (0, 0) rectangle (6, 6);
            \foreach \x/\y in {1/1, 2/3, 2/4}
                \pic at (\x + 0.5, \y + 0.5) {pacman};
            \node[diagram caption, below] at (3, -0.2) {Final state example};
        \end{scope}
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

Suppose that Pacman has no friends ($P=1$).

\begin{parts}
    \part What is the size of the minimal state space representation? Explain your answer.

    \begin{solution}
        The state $s$ is fully described with the position of Pacman and a boolean for each ghost (eaten or not). The size of the state space is therefore $M \times N \times 2^G$. We don't need to save the position of the ghosts within the state because they never change.
    \end{solution}

    \part Explain whether each of the following heuristics is admissible, consistent, neither, or both.
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
        \item $h_1(s) =$ the sum of the Manhattan distances from Pacman to every remaining ghost.
        \item $h_2(s) =$ the number of ghosts times the maximum Manhattan distance between Pacman and any of the ghosts.
        \item $h_3(s) =$ the number of remaining ghosts.
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{solution}
        A heuristic $h$ is admissible if $0 \leq h(s) \leq h^*(s)$, where $h^*(s)$ is the true forward cost of $s$. It is consistent if $h(s) \leq c(s, a, s') + h(s')$ for every successor $s' = \text{result}(s, a)$. Note that a non-admissible heuristic cannot be consistent.

        In the following state $s$, $h_1(s) = 2 + 3 + 4 = 9$ and $h_2(s) = 3 \times 4 = 12$ are larger than $h^*(s) = 4$ (not admissible). Moving Pacman one cell to the right leads to $s'$, for which $h_1(s') = 6$ and $h_2(s') = 3 \times 3 = 9$, so that $h_1(s) > c(s, a, s') + h_1(s') = 7$ and $h_2(s) > c(s, a, s') + h_2(s') = 10$ (not consistent).

        \begin{center}
            \begin{tikzpicture}[world diagram]
                \draw[grid lines] (0, 0) grid (5, 1);
                \draw[world border] (0, 0) rectangle (5, 1);
                \pic at (0.5, 0.5) {pacman};
                \foreach \x in {2, 3, 4}
                    \pic at (\x + 0.5, 0.5) {ghost};
            \end{tikzpicture}
        \end{center}

        Conversely, for any state $s$, $h_3(s)$ is a lower bound on the number of remaining turns (admissible) and a single action removes at most one ghost, so that $h_3(s) \leq c(s, a, s') + h_3(s')$ with $c(s, a, s') = 1$ (consistent).
    \end{solution}

    \uplevel{Suppose that Pacman has exactly one less friend than the number of ghosts ($P=G$).}

    \part What is the size of the minimal state space representation? Explain your answer.

    \begin{solution}
        The Pacmen are indistinguishable and occupy distinct squares, so a state is a \emph{set} of $P$ positions together with a boolean for each ghost: $\binom{MN}{P} \times 2^G$ states. Both $(M \times N)^P \times 2^G$, which distinguishes the Pacmen, and $2^{M \times N} \times 2^G$, which represents the Pacmen by one boolean per square and thus allows any number of them, are larger.
    \end{solution}

    \part Explain whether each of the following heuristics is admissible, consistent, neither, or both.
    \begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
        \item $h_4(s) =$ the largest of the Manhattan distances between each Pacman and its closest ghost.
        \item $h_5(s) =$ the smallest of the Manhattan distances between each Pacman and its closest ghost.
        \item $h_6(s) =$ the number of remaining ghosts.
        \item $h_7(s) =$ the number of remaining ghosts divided by $P$.
    \end{itemize}

    \begin{solution}
        Since $P$ Pacmen move at every turn, up to $P$ ghosts disappear per turn, which breaks the two heuristics that count turns as if a single Pacman were chasing.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item $h_4$ is not admissible, hence not consistent. Put two ghosts next to the same Pacman and the remaining Pacmen far away: two turns are enough ($h^* = 2$), while $h_4$ is the distance of the farthest Pacman to its closest ghost, which can be arbitrarily large.
            \item $h_6$ is not admissible either, hence not consistent. If every ghost is adjacent to a different Pacman, one turn ends the game ($h^* = 1$) while $h_6 = G > 1$.
            \item $h_5$ is admissible, as some Pacman must reach the ghost closest to it, which takes at least $h_5(s)$ turns, and consistent, as a turn changes each distance by at most 1.
            \item $h_7$ is admissible, as at least $G / P$ turns are needed to eliminate $G$ ghosts, and consistent, as a turn decreases the number of remaining ghosts by at most $P$.
        \end{itemize}
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}
