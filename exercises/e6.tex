\begin{thbox}{Multilayer perceptron}
    For an \thighlight{input vector} $\mathbf{x} \in \mathbb{R}^d$ and  \thighlight{activation function} $\sigma(.)$, a MLP with $L$ layers can be written as 
    \begin{align*}
        \mathbf{h}_0 &= \mathbf{x}\\
        \mathbf{h}_1 &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_1^T\mathbf{h}_0 + \mathbf{b}_1)\\
        &\vdots\\
        \mathbf{h}_L &= \sigma(\mathbf{W}_L^T\mathbf{h}_{L-1} + \mathbf{b}_L)\\
        \mathbf{y} &= \mathbf{h}_L
    \end{align*}
    with $\mathbf{h}_l \in \mathbb{R}^{q_l}$ defined as the the \thighlight{hidden vector}, $\mathbf{W}_l \in \mathbb{R}^{q_{l-1} \times q_l}$ the \thighlight{weight matrix} and $\mathbf{b}_l \in \mathbb{R}^{q_l}$ the \thighlight{bias vector} of the $l$-th layer.
\end{thbox}
\begin{thbox}{Training loop}
    For a given \thighlight{input-output training pair} $(x, y)$, a \thighlight{neural network} $\Phi_\theta(.)$ \thighlight{parameterized} by $\theta$, a \thighlight{loss function} $\mathcal{L}(.)$ and a \thighlight{learning rate} $\lambda$:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Compute all intermediate values in the network for the given input $x$ up to the output prediction $\hat{y} = \Phi_\theta(x)$. This is known as the \thighlight{forward pass}.
        \item Compute the symbolic \thighlight{gradient} of the loss w.r.t. every parameters $g_i(.) = \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \theta_i}$ using the chain rule.
        \item Evaluate the loss for your training point as $\mathcal{L}(y,\hat{y})$.
        \item Backpropagate the loss and the activations through the network to compute the gradient values $g_i$. This is known as the \thighlight{backward pass}.
        \item Update every parameter using their gradient as 
        $$
        \theta_i \leftarrow \theta_i - \lambda g_i.
        $$
    \end{enumerate}
\end{thbox}
\begin{thbox}{Universal approximation theorem}
    The \thighlight{universal approximation theorem} states that a single-hidden-layer network with a finite number of hidden units can approximate any continuous function on a compact subset of $\mathbb{R}^d$ to arbitrary accuracy.
\end{thbox}

\section{Escape game (January 2022)}

A new virtual escape game came out, and you decide to play it. You arrive in a $5 \times 5$ grid world where each cell $(x, y)$ is a room with doors leading to the adjacent rooms. The game's goal is to reach the exit room as fast as possible, but its position is unknown. Furthermore, some regions of the world are full of riddles, and crossing rooms in these regions takes longer. Fortunately, a leaderboard with the players' best times is provided, starting from a few different rooms. Due to rounding errors, you assume that the best times reported in the leaderboard are measurements affected by additive Gaussian noise $\N(0, 1)$.

\begin{table}[h]
    \centering
    \begin{tabular}{c|cc}
        \toprule
        $i$ & Starting room & Measured best time \\
        \midrule
        1 & $(4, 5)$ & $2.0$ \\
        2 & $(5, 3)$ & $3.5$ \\
        3 & $(3, 3)$ & $4.5$ \\
        4 & $(4, 1)$ & $7.0$ \\
        5 & $(1, 2)$ & $8.5$ \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{table}

From the leaderboard, you wish to learn a heuristic approximating the best time to get to the exit, starting from room $(x, y)$. You decide to use a small neural network as approximator, described by the following parametric function,
\begin{align*}
    h(x, y; \phi) & = \text{ReLU}(x w_1 + y w_2 + w_3) + \text{ReLU}(x w_4 + y w_5 + w_6) \\
    \text{ReLU}(x) & = \max(x, 0) ,
\end{align*}
where $\phi = (w_1, w_2, w_3, w_4, w_5, w_6)$ is the set of parameters/weights of the neural network.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Among the following sets of parameters ($A$, $B$ or $C$), which one would you use? Justify your answer.

    \begin{table}[h]
        \centering
        \begin{tabular}{c|cccccc}
            \toprule
            Set & $w_1$ & $w_2$ & $w_3$ & $w_4$ & $w_5$ & $w_6$ \\
            \midrule
            $\phi_A$ &$-1.5$ & $1$ & $4$ & $1$ & $-1.5$ & $6$ \\
            $\phi_B$ &$-1$ & $1.5$ & $3$ & $0$ & $-1$ & $4$ \\
            $\phi_C$ &$-2$ & $0.5$ & $4.5$ & $1.5$ & $0$ & $5$ \\
            \bottomrule
        \end{tabular}
    \end{table}

    \begin{solution}
        A set of parameters is better than another if it explains the data better, that is if the likelihood is higher. Our task is to find the set of parameters that maximizes the likelihood under the assumed probability model. In our case, the data $d$ is the leaderboard and consists of independent position-time tuples $(x_i, y_i, t_i)$. From the statement, we gather that the (best) time $t$ is a function of the starting position $(x, y)$, approximated by a neural network $h(x, y; \phi)$. We also learn that our time measurements are affected by Gaussian noise.
        \begin{align*}
            \phi_{\text{MLE}} & = \arg\max_\phi p(d | \phi) \\
            & = \arg\max_\phi \prod_{i=1}^5 p(x_i, y_i, t_i | \phi) \\ \displaybreak
            & = \arg\max_\phi \prod_{i=1}^5 p(t_i | x_i, y_i, \phi) \, p(x_i, y_i) \\
            & = \arg\max_\phi \prod_{i=1}^5 \N(t; h(x, y; \phi), 1) \\
            & = \arg\max_\phi \sum_{i=1}^5 \log \sbk*{\frac{1}{\sqrt{2 \pi}} \exp \rbk*{-\frac{(t_i - h(x_i, y_i; \phi))^2}{2}}} \\
            & = \arg\min_\phi \sum_{i=1}^5 (t_i - h(x_i, y_i; \phi))^2
        \end{align*}
        Now that we have an objective $L(\phi)$ to minimize, we can evaluate it for each of the proposed sets and select the best one.
        \begin{align*}
            L(\phi_A) & = \sum_{i=1}^5 (t_i - h(x_i, y_i; \phi_A))^2 = (2.0 - 5.5)^2 + (3.5 - 6.5)^2 + \dots = 29.75 \\
            L(\phi_B) & = 35.75 \\
            L(\phi_C) & = 205.25
        \end{align*}
        The best set is $\phi_A$.
    \end{solution}

    \item You now assume a Gaussian prior $\N(0, 1)$ on each parameter. Which set of parameters in the table above would you now choose? Justify your answer.

    \begin{solution}
        Now that we hold a prior belief on the parameters, our task is to find the most likely set of parameters given the data, that is maximum a posteriori (MAP) estimation.
        \begin{align*}
            \phi_{\text{MAP}} & = \arg\max_\phi p(\phi | d) \\
            & = \arg\max_\phi p(d | \phi) p(\phi) \\
            & = \arg\max_\phi \prod_{i=1}^5 p(x_i, y_i, t_i | \phi) \prod_{j=1}^6 p(w_j) \\
            & = \arg\max_\phi \prod_{i=1}^5 \N(t; h(x, y; \phi), 1) \prod_{j=1}^6 \N(w_j; 0, 1) \\
            & = \arg\min_\phi \sum_{i=1}^5 (t_i - h(x_i, y_i; \phi))^2 + \underbrace{\sum_{j=1}^6 (w_j)^2}_{\norm{\phi}^2}
        \end{align*}
        Here again, we have an objective $L'(\phi)$ to minimize, which we evaluate for each of the proposed sets.
        \begin{align*}
            L'(\phi_A) & = L(\phi_A) + \sum_{j=1}^6 (w_j)^2 = 29.75 + (-1.5)^2 + (1)^2 + \dots = 88.25 \\
            L'(\phi_B) & = 65.0 \\
            L'(\phi_C) & = 257.0
        \end{align*}
        The best set is $\phi_B$.
    \end{solution}

    \item Discuss the procedure you would implement on a computer to find the optimal set of parameters, had the table above not been provided.

    \begin{solution}
        If the table is not provided, it is intractable to evaluate and compare all possible sets of parameters. However, we still want to minimize the MLE (or MAP) objective $L(\phi)$. We can improve a set of parameters $\phi$ by following the opposite of the objective's gradient $\nabla_{\! \phi} L(\phi)$, \ie{} performing \emph{gradient descent} steps
        \begin{equation*}
            \phi \gets \phi - \gamma \nabla_{\! \phi} L(\phi) ,
        \end{equation*}
        where $\gamma$ is the learning rate.
    \end{solution}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Classification (CS188, Spring 2024)}
Consider the following simple neural network for binary classification. Here $x$ is a single real-valued input feature with an associated class $y$ (0 or 1). There are two weight parameters $w_1$ and $w_2$, and non-linearity functions $g_1$ and $g_2$. The network will output a value $a_2$ between 0 and 1, representing the probability of being in class 1. We will be using a loss function $\mathcal{L}$ to compare the prediction $a_2$ with the true class $y$.

\begin{figure}[h!]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/classif_2.png}
\end{figure}

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Write down the forward pass on this network, writing the output values for each node $z_1$, $a_1$, $z_2$ and $a_2$ in terms of the node’s input values.
    
    \begin{solution}
        \begin{align*}
            z_1 &= w_1 x\\
            a_1 &= g_1(z_1) = g_1(w_1 x)\\
            z_2 &= w_2 a_1 = w_2 g_1(w_1 x)\\
            a_2 &= g_2(z_2) = g_2(w_2 g_1(w_1 x))
        \end{align*}
    \end{solution}
    
    \item Derive the arguments of the loss $\mathcal{L}(a_2, y)$ in terms of the input $x$, weights $w_i$, and activation functions $g_i$.

    \begin{solution}
        The argument $a_2$ has been derived in the previous question. We have 
        $$
        a_2 = g_2(w_2 g_1(w_1 x)).
        $$
        For the other input $y$, the latter is given as input of the problem. Indeed, in supervised learning, we assume access to a training dataset made of input-output pairs $\{(x_i, y_i)\}^N$ where $y_i$ acts as a target for the network.
    \end{solution}

    \item Using the chain rule, differentiate $\mathcal{L}$ w.r.t. $w_2$. Write your expression as a product of partial derivatives at each node: i.e. the partial derivative of the node’s output with respect to its inputs. (Hint: the series of expressions you wrote in part 1 will be helpful; you may use any of those variables.)

    \begin{solution}
        $$
        \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2} = \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_2} \dfrac{\partial a_2}{\partial z_2} \dfrac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2} 
        $$
    \end{solution}

    \item Motivate a choice for the output activation function $g_2$ given the nature of the problem. Motivate also a loss function. Give the expression of the gradients of both functions w.r.t. to their inputs.

    \begin{solution}
        From the statement
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item we know that we are in a classification problem (specifically, a binary classification),
            \item we know that the output $a_2$ should be between 0 and 1 and represents the probability $p(Y = 1 \mid x)$.
        \end{enumerate}
        From (b), we have $a_2: \mathbb{R} \mapsto [0,1]$. A typical choice that fulfill this requirement is the sigmoid function $\sigma(z) = \dfrac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$. The gradient of the sigmoid w.r.t. its input is 
        $$
        \dfrac{\partial \sigma(z)}{\partial z} = \dfrac{e^{-z}}{(1 + e^{-z})^2} = \sigma(z)\sigma(-z) = \sigma(z)(1-\sigma(z)).
        $$
        For the loss function, either we remember the cross-entropy loss seen in the theoretical lecture, or we compute it from maximum likelihood estimation.\\

        The likelihood of a pair $(x, y)$ can be derived from our model as
        $$
        \begin{cases}
            \text{if } y = 1\  \text{ then }\ P(Y = y \mid x) = \text{NN}_{\theta}(x)\\
            \text{if } y = 0\  \text{ then }\ P(Y = y \mid x) = 1 -\text{NN}_{\theta}(x)
        \end{cases}
        $$
        where $\text{NN}_{\theta}$ is the neural network parameterized by $\theta = [w_1, w_2]$. Denoting $\text{NN}_{\theta}(x) = a_2$, the likelihood expression for one pair can be reformulated as 
        $$
        L(x, y; \theta) = y\log a_2 + (1-y) \log (1 - a_2).
        $$
        Since we want to maximize the likelihood, but minimize the loss, we can set the latter to
        $$
        \mathcal{L}(a_2, y) = -y\log a_2 - (1-y) \log (1 - a_2)
        $$
        which corresponds to the binary cross-entropy loss. The gradient of the loss w.r.t. $a_2$ is
        $$
        \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}(a_2, y)}{\partial a_2} = -\dfrac{y}{a_2} + \dfrac{(1-y)}{(1-a_2)} = \dfrac{a_2 - y}{a_2(1- a_2)}.
        $$
    \end{solution}
    
    \item We set the loss function to the binary cross-entropy $$\mathcal{L}(a_2, y) = -y\log a_2 - (1-y) \log (1 - a_2),$$ and $g_1$ and $g_2$ are both sigmoid functions $\sigma(z) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-z}}$. If you made this choice in the previous question, use your results to get the expression of $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2}$. Otherwise, start by expressing $\frac{\partial \sigma(z)}{\partial z}$ and $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_2}$.

    \begin{solution}
        We first miss $\frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2}$ which is
        $$
            \frac{\partial z_2}{\partial w_2} =  \frac{\partial}{\partial w_2} (w_2a_1) = a_1.
        $$
        Plugging all the partial derivatives to the expression found in part 3, we have
        \begin{align*}
            \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_2} &= \dfrac{a_2 - y}{a_2 (1 - a_2)} g_2(z_2)(1 - g_2(z_2)) a_1 \\
            &= \dfrac{a_2 - y}{a_2 (1 - a_2)} a_2(1 - a_2) a_1\\
            &= (a_2 - y) a_1
        \end{align*}
    
    \end{solution}

    \item Now use the chain rule to express $\frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1}$ as a product of partial derivatives at each node of interest.

    \begin{solution}
        $$
        \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1} = \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial a_2} \dfrac{\partial a_2}{\partial z_2} \dfrac{\partial z_2}{\partial a_1} \dfrac{\partial a_1}{\partial z_1} \dfrac{\partial z_1}{\partial w_1}
        $$
    \end{solution}

    \item Finally, write $\dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1}$ in terms of $x$, $y$, $w_i$, $a_i$, $z_i$.

    \begin{solution}
        We first need to compute 
        $$
            \frac{\partial a_1}{\partial z_1} =  g_1(z_1)(1 - g_1(z_1)) = a_1(1-a_1)
        $$
        similarly to $\frac{\partial a_2}{\partial z_2}$, and
        $$
            \frac{\partial z_1}{\partial w_1} =  \frac{\partial}{\partial w_1} (w_1x) = x.
        $$
        Then, we get 
        $$
            \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_1} = (a_2 - y)w_2 a_1(1-a_1)x.
        $$
    \end{solution}

    \item What is the gradient descent update for $w_1$ with step-size $\lambda$ in terms of the values computed above?

    \begin{solution}
        $$
        w_1 \leftarrow w_1 - \lambda (a_2 - y)w_2 a_1(1-a_1)x.
        $$
        You see here the importance of the forward pass. Iteratively, when receiving the input $x$, we compute all the intermediate activations (here, $a_1$ and $a_2$) and the loss (given $y$). Then, we propagate backward (from the output to the input) the values following the chain rule for the gradient of the loss w.r.t. parameters of interest. Finally, we update the parameters using the computed gradient.
    \end{solution}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Training loop (INFO8006, January 2024)}
Let us consider a neural network $f$ with one hidden layer taking as input a scalar $x \in \mathbb{R}$ and producing as output a scalar $y=f(x;\theta) \in \mathbb{R}$.     \item Using the data point $(x, y) = (10, -15)$ and the value of $\theta$ given above, we want to fine-tune the parameter $w_8$ of the neural network such that $f(x; \theta)$ produces a more accurate prediction of $y$.

    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Evaluate the squared error loss at the data point $(x, y) = (10, -15)$ and the current value of $\theta$.
        \begin{solution}
            \begin{align*}
                f(10, \theta) &= -17\\
            \mathcal{L}(\hat{y} &= -17, y=-15) = (17 - 15)^2 = 4    
            \end{align*}
        \end{solution}
        \item Derive an expression for the derivative of the squared error loss with respect to $w_8$.
        \begin{solution}
        The loss is
        $$
        \mathcal{L}(\hat{y}, y) = (\hat{y} - y)^2.
        $$
        We compute the gradient 
        \begin{align*}
            \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_8} &= \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \hat{y}}\dfrac{\partial \hat{y}}{\partial c_4}\dfrac{\partial c_4}{\partial c_3}\dfrac{\partial c_3}{\partial c_2}\dfrac{\partial c_2}{\partial c_1}\dfrac{\partial c_1}{\partial w_8}\\
            &= 2(\hat{y} - y)w_7\dfrac{\partial c_3}{\partial c_2}x.
        \end{align*}
        We need to compute forward activations to compute the gradient value. $\dfrac{\partial c_3}{\partial c_2}$ will be 1 if $c_2 > 0$ else $0$ by definition of the ReLU. We have
        \begin{align*}
            \hat{y} &= -17\\
            y &= -15\\
            w_7 &= 4\\
            x &= 10\\
            c_2 &= w_8x + w_9 = 5 \Rightarrow \dfrac{\partial c_3}{\partial c_2} = 1
        \end{align*}
        which gives a gradient equal to $-160$.
        \end{solution}

        \item Update the parameter $w_8$ using one step of gradient descent with a learning rate $\gamma = 0.0005$.
        
        \begin{solution}
            The gradient step is given by
            $$
            w_8 \leftarrow w_8 - \gamma \dfrac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_8}
            $$
            which gives an updated $w_8$ equal to $1.08$.
        \end{solution}
        \item Verify if the value of the loss function has decreased after the update.
        
        \begin{solution}
            With the updated $w_8$, we have $\hat{y} = -13.8$ which gives a loss equal to $1.44$. The previous loss value was $4$, we indeed decreased the loss. 
        \end{solution}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

\section{Problem Statement, Output Layer, Loss Function}

A dataset $d$ consists of $N=500$ images of cats and dogs, with exactly one cat or one dog appearing in each image. Each image has a spatial resolution of $32\times32$ pixels and is encoded in RGB format using three channels of real-valued entries. The first channel represents the red component of the image, the second channel represents the green component, and the third channel represents the blue component.


You would like to formulate a supervised learning problem in order to develop a deep learning model suited to a given task.

You are interested in three different tasks:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Assign a beauty score (a real-valued quantity) to a given image.
    \item Whether the image contains a cat or a dog.
    \item Whether each pixel of an image belongs to a cat, a dog or the background.
\end{enumerate}

For each problem, you want to identify a probabilistic model in the form $p_\theta(y \mid x)$ from a dataset that you will construct: 
$$d = \left( (x_0, y_0), (x_1, y_2), ...   \right).$$

\begin{enumerate}
    \item For each task, define the random variables $X$ and $Y$, specify their respective dimensions, and explain what $\theta$ represents.


    \begin{solution}
    For each task, $\theta$ represents the parameters of the model being developed. $X$ is the distribution of the image given as input to the model ($X \in \mathbb{R}^{3,32,32}$). 

    For the first task, $Y$ is the distribution of the beauty score associated with an image. $Y \in \mathbb{R}$.

   For the second task, $Y$ is a binary random variable indicating whether the image contains a dog (let's say $Y=1$ in this case) or a cat ($Y=0$). $Y \in \Delta^2$.

   For the third case, $Y$ is a collection of categorical random variables $P_{u,v}$, each one associated with each pixel of the image $X$:
    \[
    Y = \bigcup_{v=0}^{31} \bigcup_{u=0}^{31} P_{u,v}.
    \]
Each $P_{u,v}$ indicates whether pixel $(u,v)$ belongs to a cat, a dog, or the background. $P_{u,v} \in \Delta^3$.


    \end{solution}
\end{enumerate}

Once the task has been formulated, you construct the dataset $d$ by annotating each of the $500$ images, thereby obtaining a sample $y_i\sim Y$ associated with a sampled image $x_i\sim X$.


\begin{enumerate}
   
    \item[2.] Show that finding the parameters $\theta$ that maximize the likelihood of your model over a sample of $d$,
    \[
    p\left(
    (x_i,y_i) \mid \theta
    \right),
    \]
    is equivalent to finding the parameters $\theta$ that maximize the probability of the modelled conditional distribution over this sample,
    \[
    p\left(
   y_i \,\middle|\, x_i,\theta
    \right).
    \]
    \begin{solution}
        \begin{align*}
            \arg \max_{\theta} \left\{ p\left( (x_i, y_i) \mid \theta\right) \right\} &= \arg \max_{\theta} \left\{ p\left( y_i \mid x_i, \theta\right) p(x_i \mid \theta)\right\}\\
            &=\arg \max_{\theta} \left\{ p\left( y_i \mid x_i, \theta\right) p(x_i)\right\}\, \text{(As the model is over $Y|X$, not $X$.)}\\
            &=\arg \max_{\theta} \left\{ p\left( y_i \mid x_i, \theta\right) \right\}
        \end{align*}
    \end{solution}
\end{enumerate}

After reviewing the literature, you choose a model for $p_\theta (y | x) = p(y | x, \theta)$.

For the first task, you assume that a suitable model is to model $Y \mid X$ as a Gaussian distribution with homoscedastic variance. For the second task, a suitable model is a Bernoulli distribution. For the third task, a suitable model is a categorical distribution.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[3.] For each task, propose a suitable loss function to train your neural network. Propose also an output layer for your neural network in the form
    \[
    y = \sigma\left(W_y h + b_y\right).
    \]
Let $h$ denote the output of the layer immediately preceding the output layer, with dimension $d_o$ ($h \in \mathbb{R}^{d_o}$). Specify the dimensions of $y$, $W_y$, and $b_y$ and give an example of a suitable non-linear activation function $\sigma$ for each task.

\textit{Hint:} For each task, which parameter of the distribution $p_\theta (y |x)$ is estimated by your neural network?

\begin{solution}
    For the first task, the statistical model is:
    \begin{align*}
        Y|X &\sim \N\left(\mu (X), \sigma^2 \right)\\
        \rightarrow p(y|x) &\propto \exp\left(-\frac{1}{2}\frac{(y - \mu(x))^2}{\sigma^2} \right)
    \end{align*}
    The neural network estimates the mean of the Gaussian distribution and outputs $\hat{\mu}(X;\theta)$.
    
    For the second task:
    \begin{align*}
        Y|X &\sim \text{Bern}\left(q (X)\right)\\
        \rightarrow p(y|x) &\propto q(x)^{y}(1-q(x))^{(1-y)}
    \end{align*}
    The neural network estimates the probability that the image contains a dog and outputs $\hat{q}(X;\theta)$.

    For the third task:
    \begin{align*}
        Y|X &\sim \text{Categ}\left(q_1 (X), q_2 (X), q_3(X)\right)\\
        \rightarrow p(y|x) &\propto (q_1(x))^{\mathbb{I}(y=\text{dog} )}(q_2(x))^{\mathbb{I}(y = \text{cat})}(q_3(x))^{\mathbb{I}(y=\text{background})}
    \end{align*}
    with $q_1(x)+q_2(x)+q_3(x) = 1$ and with $\mathbb{I}(y=\alpha )$ being 1 if the pixel $y$ belongs to the class $\alpha$, $0$ otherwise.
    The neural network estimates the probability that each pixel belongs to a dog, a cat, or the background. It outputs $\hat{q}_1(X;\theta)$, $\hat{q}_2(X;\theta)$, and $\hat{q}_3(X;\theta)$, corresponding to the probabilities that a pixel belongs to a dog, a cat, or the background, respectively.

    From the statistical model, and assuming that the samples $(x_i,y_i)$ are independent and identically distributed, a loss function can be derived to maximize the likelihood of the model given the observed dataset $d$:
    \begin{align*}
        \theta_{MLE} &= \arg \max_\theta p(d|\theta) \\
        &= \arg \max_\theta p((x_0, y_0), (x_1,y_1), ... | \theta)\\
        &= \arg \max _\theta \prod_{i=0}^{500} p(x_i, y_i |\theta ) \\
        &= \arg \max _\theta \prod_{i=0}^{500} p(y_i| x_i,  \theta )  \\
        &= \arg \max _\theta \sum_{i=0}^{500} \log (p(y_i| x_i, \theta )) \\
        &= \arg \min _\theta \sum_{i=0}^{500} -\log (p(y_i| x_i, \theta )) \\
        &= \arg \min_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta,d)
    \end{align*}

    For the first task with Gaussian likelihood, the loss is the mean squared error:
    $$\mathcal{L}(\theta,d) = \frac{1}{500} \sum _{(x_i, y_i)\in d}(y_i - \hat \mu (x_i ; \theta))^2$$

    For the second task with a Bernoulli likelihood, the loss is the binary cross-entropy:
    $$\mathcal{L}(\theta,d) = -\frac{1}{500} \sum _{(x_i, y_i)\in d}y_i \log(\hat q(x_i; \theta)) + (1-y_i)\log (1- \hat q(x_i;\theta)) $$

    For the third task with categorical likelihood, the loss is the cross-entropy:
    $$\mathcal{L}(\theta,d) = -\frac{1}{500} \sum _{(x_i, y_i)\in d} \frac{1}{32 \times 32}\sum_{u=0}^{31} \sum_{v=0}^{31}\sum_{c=0}^2    y_{i, c, uv} \log(\hat q_c(x_i; \theta)) $$

    Regarding the output layer, the linear projection
    $$ W_y h + b_y$$
    must map the output $h$ of the preceding layer of the neural network to the desired output dimension.


    For the first task:
    $$W_y \in \mathbb{R}^{1, d_o} \quad b_y \in \mathbb{R} ^1$$
    For the second task:
    $$W_y \in \mathbb{R}^{1, d_o} \quad b_y \in \mathbb{R} ^1$$
    For the third task:
    $$W_y \in \mathbb{R}^{32, 32, 3, d_o} \quad b_y \in \mathbb{R} ^{ 32 , 32, 3}$$
    The non-linear activation function $\sigma$ should map the projected output to the valid range of values for $y$.
    For the first task, we can choose the identity:
    $$\sigma (\cdot) = \cdot$$
    For the second task, we can choose a sigmoid activation to map the projected values between 0 and 1:
    $$\sigma(\cdot) = \frac{1}{1+\exp(-\cdot)}$$
    For the third task, we can choose the Softmax activation to obtain logits for the three values associated for each pixel $j,i$:
    $$\sigma(y_{j,i,u}) = \frac{\exp(y_{j,i,u})}{\sum_{u=0}^2\exp(y_{j,i,u})} \quad \forall i,j,u$$
    

    

    


\end{solution}


\end{enumerate}

You want to solve a new task: estimate the age of the dog or the cat in the image. To simplify the problem, you consider the age as an unrestricted real-valued quantity. After some reflection, it becomes clear that directly inferring the age from the image is not a good idea. If the type of animal (cat or dog) were first estimated, it would be much easier to estimate the animal's age from the image and this information.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item[4.] Formulate the task in the context of supervised learning and explain all the elements appearing in $p_\theta(y \mid x)$. What mathematical quantity is estimated by the neural network ?
    
    \begin{solution}
        $y \sim Y$ is the age of the animal in the picture ($Y \in \mathbb{R}$).

        $x \sim X$ is the picture ($X \in \mathbb{R}^{3,32,32}$).
        
        $\theta$ is the parameters of the model.

        The problem is a regression problem. The neural network should give an estimate of $\E\{ Y|X\}$.
    \end{solution}
    
    \item[5.] Draw a Bayesian network modelling the image, the type of animal and its age. Find a new expression for
    $p(y \mid x) = p(y \mid x)$ that makes the effect of the type of the animal in the image explicit.

    \begin{solution}
        Let $A$ denote the random variable describing the type of animal present in the image (let's say $A=1$ if it is a dog). The animal type $A$ depends on the image $X$ and influences the age $Y$. Thus, the age $Y$ depends on both the image $X$ and the animal type $A$.

        node distance=1cm,
        every node/.style={
            circle,

    \begin{align*}
       p(y| x) &= \sum _a p(y|A=a,x)p(A=a|x)\\
       &=p(y|A=1,x)p(A=1|x) + p(y|A=0, x) (1-p(A=0 |x))
    \end{align*}
    \end{solution}

    \item[7.] Consider a large neural network designed to solve the task, divided into multiple submodules, each implemented as a separate neural network. Use the image classification model developed above as one of these submodules. Define the inputs and outputs of the additional submodules required to solve the task. Relate the resulting architecture to the mathematical expression derived above. Which submodule estimates each of the distributions appearing in this expression?
    
    \textit{Hint:} Derive an expression for $\E\{Y\mid X\}$ using the Bayesian network, explicitly accounting for the animal type $A$.

    \begin{solution}
        The neural network gives an estimate of the conditional expectation:
        \begin{align*}
            \E\{Y|X \} &= \E \{ \E \{Y|X, A, \}\}\\
            &=\sum_a \E \{Y|X, A=a \} p(A=a|X)\\
            &=\E \{Y|X, A=1,  \} p(A=1|X) + \E \{Y|X, A=0 \} (1-p(A=1|X)) 
        \end{align*}

        The expression can also be obtained directly by applying the expectation operator $\E_{Y\mid X}\{\cdot\}$ to both sides of the expression derived in Question 5.

        The expected age is therefore a weighted mean of the age inferred under the assumption that the image contains a dog and the age inferred under the assumption that it contains a cat, with each age weighted by the corresponding probability.

        The first submodule is the image classification model developed
        previously. With parameters $\phi$, it takes an image $x$ as input and estimates the probability that the image contains a dog:
        $$\hat q (x;\phi) \approx p(A=1| x) = 1 - p(A=0| x)$$

        The second submodule, with parameters $\xi$, takes the image $x$
        as input and estimates the expected age of the animal under the assumption that it is a dog:
        $$\hat \mu_d (x ; \xi) \approx \E\{Y\mid x,A=1\}$$
        
        A third submodule, with parameters $\psi$, takes the
        image $X$ as input and estimates the expected age under the assumption that it is a cat:
        $$\hat \mu_c (x ; \psi) \approx \E\{Y\mid x,A=0\}$$

        The final estimated age can then be reconstructed from the outputs of these submodules.
    \end{solution}
    
\end{enumerate}

\section{Neural Network Parameters}

Sandrine and Karl, two long-time data scientist friends, each decide to
design a neural network to solve a challenging task: compressing a high-definition color image  in a HD 720p resolution ($1280\times720$ pixels).

To do so, both decide to use an encoder network and a decoder network. The first network, the \emph{encoder}, transforms the input image into a low-dimensional latent representation which constitutes the compressed representation. The second network, the
\emph{decoder}, reconstructs the original image as faithfully as possible from this low-dimensional representation.


Sandrine decides to use a multilayer perceptron with ReLU nonlinear
activations for her encoder. Each block $i$ of her encoder therefore consists of a linear
projection followed by the nonlinear activation:
\[
    h_i = \sigma\left(W_i h_{i-1}+b_i\right).
\]

The HD color image is represented using three channels corresponding to the
red, green, and blue components. Sandrine flattens the image into a
single vector before feeding it to the network.

The dimensions of the different units throughout Sandrine's encoder are given

     \end{solution}

Karl decides to use a convolutional neural network, also with ReLU
nonlinear activations for his encoder.

Each block of the network consists of a convolutional layer with $3\times3$
kernels, followed by a ReLU activation and a max-pooling layer with a pooling area of $2\times 2$. Before being passed through each block, the input feature maps of each block are padded with a one-pixel-wide border of zeros.

The HD color image is still represented using three channels corresponding to the red, green, and blue components. The dimensions of the different units throughout Karl's encoder are given below:

\begin{figure}[H]

\begin{enumerate}
    \item[(c)] How many parameters does Karl's encoder contain? Is this network realistic to implement on a standard laptop? Assume that each parameter is encoded using $32$ bits.

    \begin{solution}
        Let the input of a block be
        \[
            h \in \mathbb{R}^{a\times b\times c},
        \]
        where $a$ denotes the number of input channels, and $b$ and $c$ denote the
        height and width of the feature maps, respectively.
        
        Before the convolution, the input is padded with a border of zeros of width
        $1$. The padded input therefore has shape
        \[
            h_p \in \mathbb{R}^{a\times(b+2)\times(c+2)}.
        \]
        
        A convolutional kernel of spatial size $3\times3$ must span all $a$ input
        channels. Consequently, each kernel has shape
        \[
            a\times3\times3
        \]
        and therefore contains
        \[
            9a
        \]
        trainable weights. If the convolutional layer contains $k$ kernels, the total
        number of weights is
        \[
            \boxed{9ak}.
        \]
        As each kernel also has a bias, the total number of trainable parameters is
        \[
            \boxed{9ak+k=k(9a+1)}.
        \]
        
        Because the input is padded by one pixel on each side and the convolution uses
        a $3\times3$ kernel, the spatial dimensions are preserved:
        \[
            (b+2)-3+1=b,
            \qquad
            (c+2)-3+1=c.
        \]
        Therefore, the output of the convolutional layer has shape
        \[
            k\times b\times c.
        \]
        
        The ReLU does not modify the dimensions. Finally, the $2\times2$ max-pooling layer reduces each spatial dimension by a factor of $2$.
        Assuming $b$ and $c$ are even, the output of the block has shape
        \[
            \boxed{k\times\frac{b}{2}\times\frac{c}{2}}.
        \]
        The number of kernels and their shapes throughout the network are:
        \begin{enumerate}
            \item $4$ kernels of shape $3\times3\times3$,
        
            \item $8$ kernels of shape $4\times3\times3$,
        
            \item $32$ kernels of shape $8\times3\times3$,
        
            \item $16$ kernels of shape $32\times3\times3$.
        \end{enumerate}
        
        The total number of parameters is therefore
        \begin{align*}
            N_{\mathrm{param}}
            &=4(3\times3\times3+1)
              +8(4\times3\times3+1)\\
            &\quad
              +32(8\times3\times3+1)
              +16(32\times3\times3+1)\\
            &=4(28)+8(37)+32(73)+16(289)\\
            &=112+296+2336+4624\\
            &=\boxed{7368}.
        \end{align*}
        Thus, using $32$ bits per parameter, the memory required to store the parameters is
        \begin{align*}
        M
        &= 7368\times32\ \text{bits}\\
        &= \frac{29\,472}{2^{20}}\ \text{MB}\\
        &\approx \boxed{0.0281\ \text{MB}}.
    \end{align*}
    Karl's encoder clearly fits in a laptop.
    \end{solution}

    \begin{solution}

            \eta = 1 - \frac{16 \times 160 \times 90}{3\times 1280 \times 720} = 92 \%
        \end{equation*}
    \end{solution}
