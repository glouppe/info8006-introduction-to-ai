\begin{recap}
    \topic{Games}
    A \term{game} is defined by
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the \term{states} $s \in \mathcal{S}$ and the \term{initial state} $s_1 \in \mathcal{S}$;
        \item the \term{actions} $\text{actions}(s) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ and the \term{transition model} $s' = \text{result}(s, a)$;
        \item a function $\text{player}(s) \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$ that says whose turn it is in state $s$;
        \item the \term{terminal states} $T \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, tested by $\text{terminal-test}(s)$, \ie{} $s \in T$;
        \item a \term{utility} $\text{utility}(s, p)$, the payoff of player $p$ when the game ends in $s \in T$.
    \end{itemize}
    The \term{game tree} is defined by $s_1$, $\text{actions}$ and $\text{result}$. A solution is a \term{strategy} $\pi_p : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}$, a move for every state the opponent can lead to.

    \topic{Zero-sum games}
    In a \term{zero-sum} game, the total payoff is constant. Below, the game is deterministic, turn-taking, two-player, zero-sum and of perfect information: MAX maximizes the utility, MIN minimizes it, and MAX moves first.

    \topic{Minimax}
    The \term{minimax value} of $s$ is the best payoff MAX can secure against an optimal MIN,
    \[
        \text{minimax}(s) = \begin{cases}
            \text{utility}(s) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            \max_{a} \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_{a} \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MIN plays,}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    over $a \in \text{actions}(s)$, and the optimal move is $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a))$. Time $O(b^m)$, space $O(bm)$.

    \topic{$\alpha$-$\beta$ pruning}
    $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the best values found so far for MAX and MIN on the path from the root: a MIN node stops as soon as its value $v \leq \alpha$, a MAX node as soon as $v \geq \beta$. The minimax values are unchanged and, with a perfect move ordering, $O(b^{m/2})$ nodes are examined. A \term{transposition table} stores the values of the states already evaluated, which other move sequences reach again.

    \topic{H-Minimax}
    Under time constraints, an \term{evaluation function} $\text{eval}(s)$ replaces the utility and a \term{cutoff test} at depth $d$ replaces the terminal test,
    \[
        \text{h-minimax}(s, d) = \begin{cases}
            \text{eval}(s) & \text{if cutoff} \\
            \max_{a} \text{h-minimax}(\text{result}(s, a), d + 1) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_{a} \text{h-minimax}(\text{result}(s, a), d + 1) & \text{if MIN plays.}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    The evaluation should order states as their minimax values do, and the cutoff apply to \term{quiescent} states only; what lies beyond the depth limit is hidden (\term{horizon effect}).

    \topic{Expectiminimax}
    \term{Chance nodes} map a state-action pair to its outcomes $r$, of probabilities $P(r)$. With $v(s) = \text{expectiminimax}(s)$,
    \[
        v(s) = \begin{cases}
            \text{utility}(s) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            \max_a v(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_a v(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MIN plays} \\
            \sum_r P(r) \, v(\text{result}(s, r)) & \text{if CHANCE plays.}
        \end{cases}
    \]

    \topic{Monte Carlo tree search}
    Each node $n$ keeps the wins $Q(n, p)$ of player $p$ over the random playouts through $n$ and its visits $N(n)$. A round does \term{selection}, \term{expansion}, \term{simulation} and \term{backpropagation}; selection picks the child $n'$ that maximizes
    \[
        \frac{Q(n', p)}{N(n')} + c \sqrt{\frac{\log N(n)}{N(n')}}.
    \]
\end{recap}

\titledquestion{Tic-tac-toe \aima{5}}

Tic-Tac-Toe is a game for two players, X and O, who take turns marking the cells of a $3 \times 3$ grid, X moving first. The player who succeeds in placing three of their marks in a straight line (horizontal, vertical or diagonal) wins the game. If neither of the players wins before the grid is full, it is a draw.

We consider X as the max player and O as the min player. We define $X_n$ as the number of rows, columns or diagonals with exactly $n$ X's and no O's. Similarly, $O_n$ is the number of rows, columns, or diagonals with just $n$ O's. A position $s$ is terminal if $X_3(s) \geq 1$, $O_3(s) \geq 1$ or if the grid is full. The utility function assigns $+1$, $-1$ or $0$ to such position, respectively. For non-terminal positions, we use an evaluation function defined as $\text{eval}(s) = 3 X_2(s) + X_1(s) - 3 O_2(s) - O_1(s)$.

\begin{parts}
    \part Define the search problem associated with the Tic-Tac-Toe game.

    \begin{solution}
        Let a state of the game be a matrix $s = (s_{ij}) \in \cbk{-1, 0, 1}^{3 \times 3}$, where the values $-1$, $0$ and $1$ respectively denote O, empty and X cells. X is MAX and O is MIN.
        \begin{description}
            \item[Initial state]
            \begin{equation*}
                s_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{pmatrix}
            \end{equation*}
            \item[Player function] X moves when there are as many X's as O's on the grid, and O otherwise,
            \begin{equation*}
                \text{player}(s) = \begin{cases}
                    \text{MAX} & \text{if } \sum_{i, j} s_{ij} = 0 \\
                    \text{MIN} & \text{otherwise.}
                \end{cases}
            \end{equation*}
            \item[Actions] An action is represented by the position $(i, j)$ of an empty cell in the grid. Then, the set of possible actions is $\text{actions}(s) = \cbk{(i, j): s_{ij} = 0}$.
            \item[Transition model] For an action $(i, j) \in \text{actions}(s)$, $\text{result}(s, (i, j)) = s'$ is the same as $s$ except that $s'_{ij} = 1$ if $\text{player}(s) = \text{MAX}$ and $-1$ otherwise.
            \item[Terminal test] $\text{terminal-test}(s)$ is true if $X_3(s) + O_3(s) \geq 1$ or $\prod_{i,j} s_{ij} \neq 0$, false otherwise.
            \item[Utility function] $\text{utility}(s, \text{MAX}) = 1$ if $X_3(s) \geq 1$, $-1$ if $O_3(s) \geq 1$ and $0$ otherwise.
        \end{description}
    \end{solution}

    \part Approximately how many possible game states of Tic-Tac-Toe are there?

    \begin{solution}
        Every cell being empty, X or O, there are at most $3^{3 \times 3} = \num{19683}$ states, unreachable ones included, and at most $\fact{9} = \num{362880}$ games, as many games end before the grid is full. The exact counts are \num{5478} reachable states and \num{255168} games.

    \part This 4-cell game can be generalized to $n$ cells for any $n > 2$. Prove that $A$ wins if $n$ is even and loses if $n$ is odd.

    \begin{solution}
        Let $P$ be $A$ if $n$ is even and $B$ if $n$ is odd, and $Q$ the other player. We show that $P$ wins by always moving towards its goal, the cell where $Q$ starts. Let $d$ be the number of free cells between the two tokens, as long as they have not crossed.
        \begin{itemize}
            \item \emph{$Q$ never jumps before the crossing.} $d = n - 2$ at the first turn of $A$ and $d = n - 3$ at the first turn of $B$, so $d$ is even at the first turn of $P$. Each move of $P$ decreases $d$ by 1 and each move of $Q$ changes it by $\pm 1$, so $d$ is even at every turn of $P$ and odd at every turn of $Q$. $Q$ therefore never faces an adjacent token, the only case where it could jump.
            \item \emph{$P$ can always move forward.} It steps into a free cell if $d > 0$ and jumps over $Q$ if $d = 0$. The jump is impossible only if $Q$ stands on the goal of $P$, at the end of the board, but $Q$ could only have moved there from the cell that $P$ now occupies, or by jumping over $P$, and neither is possible.
            \item \emph{The crossing.} Let $P$ jump on its $(m + 1)$-th move, after $m$ moves of one cell. Meanwhile, $Q$ made $m$ moves (if $P = A$) or $m + 1$ (if $P = B$), each bringing it at most one cell closer. The tokens started $n - 2$ free cells apart, so $n - 2 \leq 2m + 1$, \ie{} $n - 3 - m \leq m$. After the jump, $P$ is $n - 3 - m$ cells from its goal and $Q$, to move, is $m + 1$ cells from its own.
            \item \emph{The race.} No token stands between $Q$ and its goal any more, so $Q$ needs at least $m + 1$ moves, while $P$ reaches its goal in $n - 3 - m \leq m$ moves, before the $(m + 1)$-th move of $Q$. $Q$ can get in front of $P$ again only by jumping over it, two cells away from its own goal; $P$ then jumps over $Q$ in turn or, if $Q$ landed on the goal of $P$, steps back and jumps over $Q$ once $Q$, forced to move, has left that cell.
        \end{itemize}
        $P$ thus wins: $A$ wins if $n$ is even and $B$ if $n$ is odd.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Chess and transposition table \aima{5}}

Suppose you have a chess program that can evaluate 16 million nodes per second.

\begin{parts}
    \part Decide on a compact representation of a game state for storage in a transposition table.

    \begin{solution}
        There are 32 pieces, each on one of the 64 squares of the board, which takes \qty{6}{bits} ($2^6 = 64$); a captured piece can be put on the square of its own king, which is never captured. This makes $32 \times 6 = 192$ bits, \ie{} \qty{24}{B} per state. This is a lower bound: a complete entry also stores the side to move, the castling and en passant rights, the promoted pieces, which fixed piece slots cannot encode, and the value found by the search with its depth.
    \end{solution}

    \part About how many entries can you fit in a \qty{4}{\giga B} in-memory table?

    \begin{solution}
        We can store roughly $\num{4e9} \times \frac{1}{24} \approx \num{1.7e8}$ states in the table.
    \end{solution}

    \part Will that be enough for the three minutes of search allocated for one move?

    \begin{solution}
        It is not enough to store all $\num{16e6} \times 3 \times 60 = \num{2.88e9}$ evaluated nodes, about 17 times more.
    \end{solution}

    \part How many table lookups can you do in the time it would take to do one evaluation? Suppose that you have a \qty{3.2}{\giga\hertz} machine and that it takes 20 operations to do one lookup on the transposition table.

    \begin{solution}
        We can perform
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\num{3.2e9}}{\num{16e6}} \times \frac{1}{20} = 10
        \end{equation*}
        table lookups in the same amount of time as a single evaluation. A lookup thus costs a tenth of an evaluation, and a hit saves at least that evaluation: the table pays off as soon as more than one lookup in ten finds its state.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

        \foreach \i/\x in {1/0.5, 2/2.5, 3/4.5, 4/6.5} {
            \node[player node] (Z\i) at (\x, -2) {3};
        }
        \foreach \i/\u in {0/{1, 6, 3}, 1/{4, 2, 5}, 2/{7, 1, 1}, 3/{2, 5, 4}, 4/{3, 3, 6}, 5/{6, 4, 2}, 6/{5, 1, 3}, 7/{0, 7, 2}} {
            \node[leaf] (L\i) at (\i, -3) {$(\u)$};
        }
        \draw[link] (R) -- (X) (R) -- (Y) (X) -- (Z1) (X) -- (Z2) (Y) -- (Z3) (Y) -- (Z4)
            (Z1) -- (L0) (Z1) -- (L1) (Z2) -- (L2) (Z2) -- (L3)
            (Z3) -- (L4) (Z3) -- (L5) (Z4) -- (L6) (Z4) -- (L7);
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{parts}
    \part Back up the utilities to the root. Which move does player 1 choose? Is the game zero-sum?

    \begin{solution}
        The nodes of player 3 are worth, from left to right, $(4, 2, 5)$, $(2, 5, 4)$, $(3, 3, 6)$ and $(5, 1, 3)$, those of player 2 are worth $(2, 5, 4)$ and $(3, 3, 6)$, and the root $(3, 3, 6)$: player 1 moves right. The game is not zero-sum, as the total utility of the leaves varies from 9 to 12.
    \end{solution}

    \part Player 1 now assumes that the two other players play against it: it runs minimax on $u_1$, as MAX, with players 2 and 3 as MIN. Which move does it choose, and what does it get if players 2 and 3 in fact play as in (a)?

    \begin{solution}
        The nodes of player 2 are worth $\min(1, 4, 7, 2) = 1$ on the left and $\min(3, 6, 5, 0) = 0$ on the right, so player 1 moves left. Players 2 and 3 then lead to $(2, 5, 4)$, and player 1 gets 2 instead of 3. Minimax guarantees at least 1, but its assumption is wrong here: players 2 and 3 maximize their own utilities, not the loss of player 1.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Evaluation functions with chance nodes}

Consider the game tree below, in which MAX chooses between two chance nodes, with the probabilities of the outcomes on the edges.

\begin{center}
    \begin{tikzpicture}[game diagram, x=1.5cm, y=1.8cm]
        \node[max node] (R) at (1.5, 0) {};
        \node[chance node] (A) at (0.5, -1) {};
        \node[chance node] (B) at (2.5, -1) {};
        \node[leaf] (A1) at (-0.1, -2) {2};
        \node[leaf] (A2) at (1.1, -2) {4};
        \node[leaf] (B1) at (1.9, -2) {1};
        \node[leaf] (B2) at (3.1, -2) {6};
        \draw[link] (R.south) -- node[tag, left] {left} (A) (R.south) -- node[tag, right] {right} (B)
            (A) -- node[cost] {0.5} (A1) (A) -- node[cost] {0.5} (A2)
            (B) -- node[cost] {0.8} (B1) (B) -- node[cost] {0.2} (B2);
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{parts}
    \part Compute the expectiminimax value of each node. Which move does MAX choose?

    \begin{solution}
        The chance nodes are worth $0.5 \times 2 + 0.5 \times 4 = 3$ and $0.8 \times 1 + 0.2 \times 6 = 2$, so MAX moves left, for a value of 3.
    \end{solution}

    \part Both $f(x) = 2x + 1$ and $g(x) = 2^x$ preserve the order of the leaves. Replace every leaf value $x$ by $f(x)$, then by $g(x)$. Which move does MAX choose in each case?

    \begin{solution}
        With $f$, the leaves become 5, 9, 3 and 13, and the chance nodes are worth 7 and $0.8 \times 3 + 0.2 \times 13 = 5$: MAX still moves left. With $g$, the leaves become 4, 16, 2 and 64, and the chance nodes are worth 10 and $0.8 \times 2 + 0.2 \times 64 = 14.4$: MAX now moves right.
    \end{solution}

    \part Would $g$ change the move if the chance nodes were MIN nodes? What does this say of the evaluation functions of H-Minimax, with and without chance nodes?

    \begin{solution}
        No: the MIN nodes are worth $\min(2, 4) = 2$ and $\min(1, 6) = 1$, and $\min(4, 16) = 4$ and $\min(2, 64) = 2$ with $g$, so MAX moves left in both cases. Minimax only compares values, so any order-preserving transformation of the evaluation function leaves its decisions unchanged. Expectations add values, so with chance nodes the evaluation function must be a positive linear transformation of the expected utility, such as $f$ and unlike $g$.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Selection in Monte Carlo tree search}

During the selection step of Monte Carlo tree search, player $p$ is to move at a node $n$ that has been visited $N(n) = 20$ times. The wins of $p$ and the visits of the three children of $n$ are given below.

\begin{center}
    \small
    \begin{tabular}{lccc}
        \toprule
        Child $n'$ & $n'_1$ & $n'_2$ & $n'_3$ \\
        \midrule
        Wins $Q(n', p)$ & 8 & 3 & 1 \\
        Visits $N(n')$ & 12 & 6 & 2 \\
        \bottomrule
    \end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{parts}
    \part Which child does UCB1 select with $c = 0$? With $c = 1$? Use the natural logarithm.

    \begin{solution}
        The exploitation terms $Q(n', p) / N(n')$ are $0.667$, $0.5$ and $0.5$, and the exploration terms $\sqrt{\log N(n) / N(n')}$ are $0.500$, $0.707$ and $1.224$, with $\log 20 = 3.00$. With $c = 0$, UCB1 selects $n'_1$, the best win rate. With $c = 1$, the scores are $1.166$, $1.207$ and $1.724$, and UCB1 selects $n'_3$, the least visited child.
    \end{solution}

    \part For which values of $c$ does UCB1 select $n'_1$?

    \begin{solution}
        $n'_1$ beats $n'_3$ if $0.667 + 0.500 c \geq 0.5 + 1.224 c$, \ie{} $c \leq 0.23$, and beats $n'_2$ if $0.667 + 0.500 c \geq 0.5 + 0.707 c$, \ie{} $c \leq 0.81$. UCB1 thus selects $n'_1$ for $c \leq 0.23$: the smaller $c$, the more UCB1 exploits the best win rate so far, and the larger $c$, the more it explores the children visited least.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}
