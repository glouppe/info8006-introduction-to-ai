\begin{recap}
    \topic{Games}
    A \term{game} is the search problem of lecture 2 with the states $s \in \mathcal{S}$, the initial state $s_1$, the actions $\text{actions}(s) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ and the transition model $\text{result}(s, a)$, where
    \begin{itemize}
        \item $\text{player}(s) \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$ says whose turn it is in $s$;
        \item the goal states become the \term{terminal states} $T \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, tested by $\text{terminal-test}(s)$, \ie{} $s \in T$;
        \item the path cost becomes a \term{utility} $\text{utility}(s, p)$, the payoff of player $p$ when the game ends in $s \in T$.
    \end{itemize}
    Together, $s_1$, $\text{actions}$ and $\text{result}$ define the \term{game tree}. A solution is a \term{strategy}, a policy $\pi_p : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}$ that gives a move for every state the opponent can lead to.

    \topic{Zero-sum games}
    In a \term{zero-sum} game, the total payoff to all players is constant. We assume a deterministic, turn-taking, two-player zero-sum game with perfect information, whose players MAX and MIN maximize and minimize the same $\text{utility}$; MAX moves first.

    \topic{Minimax}
    The \term{minimax value} of $s$ is the largest payoff MAX can achieve against an optimal MIN,
    \[
        \text{minimax}(s) = \begin{cases}
            \text{utility}(s) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            \max_{a} \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_{a} \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MIN plays,}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    over $a \in \text{actions}(s)$, and the optimal move is $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a))$. Time $O(b^m)$, space $O(bm)$.

    \topic{$\alpha$-$\beta$ pruning}
    $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the best values found so far for MAX and MIN along the path from the root. A MIN node stops expanding its children as soon as its value $v \leq \alpha$, a MAX node as soon as $v \geq \beta$. Pruning leaves the minimax values unchanged; with a perfect ordering of the moves, it examines $O(b^{m/2})$ nodes.

    \topic{H-Minimax}
    Under time constraints, the utility is replaced by an \term{evaluation function} $\text{eval}(s)$ and the terminal test by a \term{cutoff test} $\text{cutoff-test}(s, d)$ at depth $d$,
    \[
        \text{h-minimax}(s, d) = \begin{cases}
            \text{eval}(s) & \text{if cutoff} \\
            \max_{a} \text{h-minimax}(\text{result}(s, a), d + 1) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_{a} \text{h-minimax}(\text{result}(s, a), d + 1) & \text{if MIN plays.}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    Ideally, $\text{eval}$ orders the states as their minimax values do. The cutoff applies to \term{quiescent} states; consequences beyond the depth limit are hidden by the \term{horizon effect}.

    \topic{Expectiminimax}
    In \term{stochastic games}, \term{chance nodes} map a state-action pair to its outcomes $r$, with probabilities $P(r)$. Writing $v(s) = \text{expectiminimax}(s)$,
    \[
        v(s) = \begin{cases}
            \text{utility}(s) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            \max_a v(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_a v(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MIN plays} \\
            \sum_r P(r) \, v(\text{result}(s, r)) & \text{if CHANCE plays.}
        \end{cases}
    \]

    \topic{Monte Carlo tree search}
    Each node $n$ keeps the number of wins $Q(n, p)$ of player $p$ over the random playouts through $n$, and its number of visits $N(n)$. A round does \term{selection}, \term{expansion}, \term{simulation} and \term{backpropagation}; selection picks the child $n'$ of $n$ that maximizes
    \[
        \frac{Q(n', p)}{N(n')} + c \sqrt{\frac{\log N(n)}{N(n')}}.
    \]
\end{recap}

\titledquestion{Tic-tac-toe \aima{5}}

Tic-Tac-Toe is a game for two players, X and O, who take turns marking the cells of a $3 \times 3$ grid. The player who succeeds in placing three of their marks in a straight line (horizontal, vertical or diagonal) wins the game. If neither of the players wins before the grid is full, it is a draw.

We consider X as the max player and O as the min player. We define $X_n$ as the number of rows, columns or diagonals with exactly $n$ X's and no O's. Similarly, $O_n$ is the number of rows, columns, or diagonals with just $n$ O's. A position $s$ is terminal if $X_3(s) \geq 1$, $O_3(s) \geq 1$ or if the grid is full. The utility function assigns $+1$, $-1$ or $0$ to such position, respectively. For non-terminal positions, we use an evaluation function defined as $\text{eval}(s) = 3 X_2(s) + X_1(s) - 3 O_2(s) - O_1(s)$.

\begin{parts}
    \part Define the search problem associated with the Tic-Tac-Toe game.

    \begin{solution}
        Let a state of the game be a matrix $s = (s_{ij}) \in \cbk{-1, 0, 1}^{3 \times 3}$, where the values $-1$, $0$ and $1$ respectively denote O, empty and X cells.
        \begin{description}
            \item[Initial state]
            \begin{equation*}
                s_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{pmatrix}
            \end{equation*}
            \item[Player function] The next player should be X if there are as many X's as O's on the grid and O otherwise. Choosing $+1$ for X and $-1$ for O,
            \begin{equation*}
                \text{player}(s) = \begin{cases}
                    +1 & \text{if } \sum_{i, j} s_{ij} = 0 \\
                    -1 & \text{otherwise}
                \end{cases}
            \end{equation*}
            \item[Actions] An action is represented by the position $(i, j)$ of an empty cell in the grid. Then, the set of possible actions is $\text{actions}(s) = \cbk{(i, j): s_{ij} = 0}$.
            \item[Transition model] For an action $(i, j) \in \text{actions}(s)$, $\text{result}(s, (i, j)) = s'$ is the same as $s$ except that $s'_{ij} = \text{player}(s)$.
            \item[Terminal test] $\text{terminal-test}(s)$ is true if $X_3(s) + O_3(s) \geq 1$ or $\prod_{i,j} s_{ij} \neq 0$, false otherwise.
            \item[Utility function] $\text{utility}(s) = 1$ if $X_3(s) \geq 1$, $-1$ if $O_3(s) \geq 1$ and $0$ otherwise.
        \end{description}
    \end{solution}

    \part Approximately how many possible game states of Tic-Tac-Toe are there?

    \begin{solution}
        If we disregard unreachable states, we have $3^{3 \times 3} = \num{19683}$ possible states and $\fact{9} = \num{362880}$ possible games.
    \end{solution}

    \part Using the H-Minimax algorithm, annotate your tree with the backed-up values for the positions at depths 1 and 0, and use those values to choose the optimal starting move.

    \begin{solution}

            \caption{Nodes in red corresponds to nodes that would have been pruned, \ie{} not evaluated, if $\alpha$-$\beta$ pruning was applied, assuming the nodes are generated in the optimal order for $\alpha$-$\beta$ pruning.}

    \part Is this evaluation function a good heuristic? If not, provide one or more states $s$ for which $\text{eval}(s)$ is misleading.

    \begin{solution}

\titledquestion{21 misery game \source{Exam, January 2019}}

\begin{parts}
    \part Define the search problem associated with the 2-player version of the \enquote{21} game.

    \begin{solution}
        Let a state of the game be a pair $s = (v, p) \in \mathbb{Z} \times \cbk{0, 1}$, where $v$ is the current value of the counter and $p$ the player to play next.
        \begin{description}
            \item[Initial state] $s_1 = (1, 0)$.
            \item[Player function] $\text{player}(s = (v, p)) = p$.

\titledquestion{Leapfrog \aima{5}}

Consider the following two-player turn-taking game which initial configuration is shown in the figure below. Player $A$ moves first. Each player must move their token to an adjacent free cell in either direction. If the opponent occupies an adjacent cell, then a player may jump over the opponent to the next free cell, if any. For example, if $A$ is on $3$ and $B$ is on $2$, then $A$ may move back to $1$. The game ends when a player reaches the opposite end of the board. If player $A$ reaches cell $4$ first, then the value of the game to $A$ is $+1$; if player $B$ reaches cell $1$ first, then the value of the game to $A$ is $-1$.

        \node at (0.5, -0.5) {1};
        \node at (1.5, -0.5) {2};
        \node at (2.5, -0.5) {3};
        \node at (3.5, -0.5) {4};
        \node[draw, circle] at (0.5, 0.5) {$A$};
        \node[draw, circle] at (3.5, 0.5) {$B$};
    \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}

\begin{parts}
    \part Define the search problem associated with this game.

    \begin{solution}
        Let a state of the game be a tuple $s = (i, j, p) \in \cbk{1, 2, 3, 4}^2 \times \cbk{0, 1}$, where $i$ and $j$ are the respective positions of $A$ and $B$ and $p$ is $0$ when it is $A$'s turn and $1$ when it is $B$'s.
        \begin{description}
            \item[Initial state] $s_1 = (1, 4, 0)$.
            \item[Player function] $\text{player}(s = (i, j, p)) = p$.
            \item[Actions] $\text{actions}(s) \subseteq \cbk{\text{left}, \text{right}}$. The \enquote{left} (resp. \enquote{right}) action is only available if there is a free cell to the left (resp. right) of the current player.
            \item[Transition model] For an available action $a \in \text{actions}(s)$,
            \begin{equation*}
                \text{result}(s = (i, j, p), a) = \begin{cases}
                    (\text{next}(i, j), j, 1) & \text{if } p = 0 \text{ and } a = \text{right} \\
                    (\text{prev}(i, j), j, 1) & \text{if } p = 0 \text{ and } a = \text{left} \\
                    (i, \text{next}(j, i), 0) & \text{if } p = 1 \text{ and } a = \text{right} \\
                    (i, \text{prev}(j, i), 0) & \text{if } p = 1 \text{ and } a = \text{left}
                \end{cases}
            \end{equation*}
            where
            \begin{align*}
                \text{next}(x, y) & = \begin{cases}
                    y + 1 & \text{if } x + 1 = y \\
                    x + 1 & \text{otherwise}
                \end{cases} \\
                \text{prev}(x, y) & = \begin{cases}
                    y - 1 & \text{if } x - 1 = y \\
                    x - 1 & \text{otherwise}
                \end{cases} .
            \end{align*}
            \item[Terminal test] $\text{terminal-test}(s = (i, j, p))$ is true if $i = 4$ or $j = 1$, false otherwise.
            \item[Utility function]
            \begin{equation*}
                \text{utility}(s = (i, j, p)) = \begin{cases}
                    +1 & \text{if } i = 4 \\
                    -1 & \text{if } j = 1
                \end{cases} .
            \end{equation*}
        \end{description}
    \end{solution}

        The Minimax algorithm is designed to work on finite acyclic game trees. In our case, the tree is not acyclic, which leads the standard Minimax algorithm into infinite loops.

        Our strategy is equivalent to replacing \enquote{?} by any value strictly between $-1$ and $+1$, which only works for this game tree.     \part This 4-cell game can be generalized to $n$ cells for any $n > 2$. Prove that $A$ wins if $n$ is even and loses if $n$ is odd.

    \begin{solution}
        We already know that $A$ wins if $n = 4$ and it is quick to verify that $B$ wins if $n = 3$. For $n \geq 5$, we notice that the three first states are $(1, n, 0)$, $(2, n, 1)$ and $(2, n - 1, 0)$. For optimal players, the state $(2, n - 1, 0)$ is equivalent (in the sense that the winner is the same) to the initial state of the game with $n - 2$ cells. Likewise, the outcome for $n$ cells is the same as for $n + 2$ cells and, by induction, for $n + 2k$ ($k \geq 0$) cells. Therefore, we have that $A$ wins games with $4 + 2k$ cells (even) and loses those with $3 + 2k$ cells (odd).
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Chess and transposition table \aima{5}}

Suppose you have a chess program that can evaluate 16 million nodes per second.

\begin{parts}
    \part Decide on a compact representation of a game state for storage in a transposition table.

    \begin{solution}
        There are 32 pieces and we need to specify their positions in a $8 \times 8$ board. If a piece is not on the board anymore, we can fix its position as the position of the King. This position can be stored in \qty{6}{bits} ($2^6 = 64$), for a total of \qty{24}{B} per state.
    \end{solution}

    \part About how many entries can you fit in a \qty{4}{\giga B} in-memory table?

    \begin{solution}
        We can store roughly $\num{4e9} \times \frac{1}{24} \approx \num{160e6}$ states in the table.
    \end{solution}

    \part Will that be enough for the three minutes of search allocated for one move?

    \begin{solution}
        It is not enough to store all $\num{16e6} \times 3 \times 60 = \num{2.880e9}$ evaluated nodes.
    \end{solution}

    \part How many table lookups can you do in the time it would take to do one evaluation? Suppose that you have a \qty{3.2}{\giga\hertz} machine and that it takes 20 operations to do one lookup on the transposition table.

    \begin{solution}
        We can perform
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\num{3.2e9}}{\num{16e6}} \times \frac{1}{20} = 10
        \end{equation*}
        table lookups in the same amount of time as a single evaluation. This demonstrates the importance of transposition tables.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\begin{quiz}
    \item In Monte Carlo Tree Search (MCTS), in the formula
    \begin{equation*}
        \frac{Q(n', p)}{N(n')} + c \sqrt{\frac{\log N(n)}{N(n')}},
    \end{equation*}
    which of the following is true?
    \begin{enumerate}[label=$\bigcirc$]
        \item \correct{The first term encourages the exploitation of higher-reward nodes, while the second encourages the exploration of less-visited nodes.}
        \item The first term encourages the exploration of less-visited nodes, while the second term encourages the exploitation of higher-reward nodes.
        \item The first term encourages the exploitation of highly-visited nodes, while the second term encourages the exploration of lesser-rewarding nodes.
        \item The first term encourages the exploration of lesser-rewarding nodes, while the second term encourages the exploitation of highly-visited nodes.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item In adversarial search,
    \begin{enumerate}[label=$\bigcirc$]
        \item The horizon effect arises when the search is stuck in a cycle.
        \item The horizon effect arises when the evaluation function is perfect.
        \item The deeper in the tree the evaluation function is buried, the more the quality of the evaluation matters.
        \item \correct{If not looked deep enough, bad moves may appear as good moves, because their consequences are hidden beyond the search horizon.}
    \end{enumerate}

    \item A quiescent state is
    \begin{enumerate}[label=$\bigcirc$]
        \item A state in which the game will loop indefinitely.
        \item A state in which an agent is stuck.
        \item \correct{A state in which the outcome of a game is unlikely to vary a lot in the near future.}
        \item A state in which the MAX agent is certain to win.
    \end{enumerate}

    \item Minimax algorithm
    \begin{enumerate}[label=$\bigcirc$]
        \item \correct{Cannot fail more against a sub-optimal agent than an optimal one.}
        \item \correct{Can fail more often if the opponent is sub-optimal but predictable and that we underestimate the opponent strategy.}
        \item \correct{Does not ensure that the MAX agent always wins.}
        \item \correct{All of the above.}
    \end{enumerate}
\end{quiz}
