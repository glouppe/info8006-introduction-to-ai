\begin{recap}
    \topic{Games}
    A \term{game} is defined by
    \begin{itemize}
        \item the \term{states} $s \in \mathcal{S}$ and the \term{initial state} $s_1 \in \mathcal{S}$;
        \item the \term{actions} $\text{actions}(s) \subseteq \mathcal{A}$ available in state $s$;
        \item the \term{transition model} $s' = \text{result}(s, a)$, the state reached by taking $a$ in $s$;
        \item the player to move, $\text{player}(s) \in \{1, \ldots, N\}$;
        \item the \term{terminal states} $T \subseteq \mathcal{S}$, tested by $\text{terminal-test}(s)$;
        \item the \term{utility} $\text{utility}(s, p)$ of player $p$ in $s \in T$.
    \end{itemize}
    $s_1$, $\text{actions}$ and $\text{result}$ define the \term{game tree}. A solution is a \term{strategy} $\pi_p : \mathcal{S} \to \mathcal{A}$, a move for every state the opponent can lead to.

    \topic{Zero-sum games}
    A game is \term{zero-sum} if $\sum_{p=1}^N \text{utility}(s, p) = c$ for all $s \in T$. With two players, called MAX and MIN, $\text{utility}(s, \text{MIN}) = c - \text{utility}(s, \text{MAX})$; we write $\text{utility}(s)$ for $\text{utility}(s, \text{MAX})$, which MAX maximizes and MIN minimizes. Below, games are deterministic, turn-taking, two-player, zero-sum and of perfect information, and MAX moves first.

    \topic{Minimax}
    The \term{minimax value} of $s$ is the payoff MAX can secure against an optimal MIN,
    \[
        \text{minimax}(s) = \begin{cases}
            \text{utility}(s) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            \max_{a} \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_{a} \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a)) & \text{if MIN plays,}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    with $a \in \text{actions}(s)$. The optimal move is $\pi^*(s) = \arg\max_a \text{minimax}(\text{result}(s, a))$. Time $O(b^m)$, space $O(bm)$.

    \topic{$\alpha$-$\beta$ pruning}
    $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are the best values found so far for MAX and MIN on the path from the root; a MIN node stops once $v \leq \alpha$, a MAX node once $v \geq \beta$. Minimax values are unchanged. With a perfect move ordering, $O(b^{m/2})$ nodes are examined. A \term{transposition table} stores $\text{minimax}(s)$ for the states already evaluated.

    \topic{H-Minimax}
    An \term{evaluation function} $\text{eval}(s)$ replaces the utility, and a \term{cutoff test} at depth $d$ the terminal test,
    \[
        \text{h-minimax}(s, d) = \begin{cases}
            \text{eval}(s) & \text{if cutoff} \\
            \max_{a} \text{h-minimax}(\text{result}(s, a), d + 1) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_{a} \text{h-minimax}(\text{result}(s, a), d + 1) & \text{if MIN plays.}
        \end{cases}
    \]
    The evaluation should order states as their minimax values do, and the cutoff apply to \term{quiescent} states only. Beyond the cutoff, consequences are hidden (\term{horizon effect}). A deeper search compensates an inaccurate evaluation, if it is more accurate near the end of the game; the converse does not hold.

    \topic{Multi-player games}
    With $N > 2$ players, or without the zero-sum assumption, minimax backs up a vector of utilities $v(s) \in \mathbb{R}^N$; the player to move maximizes its own component,
    \[
        v(s) = \begin{cases}
            \left(\text{utility}(s, 1), \ldots, \text{utility}(s, N)\right) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            v(\text{result}(s, a^*)) & \text{otherwise,}
        \end{cases}
        \qquad a^* = \arg\max_a v_{\text{player}(s)}(\text{result}(s, a)).
    \]

    \topic{Expectiminimax}
    A \term{chance node} $(s, a)$ leads to the next state $s'$ with probability $P(s' \mid s, a)$. With $v(s) = \text{expectiminimax}(s)$,
    \[
        v(s) = \begin{cases}
            \text{utility}(s) & \text{if } s \in T \\
            \max_a q(s, a) & \text{if MAX plays} \\
            \min_a q(s, a) & \text{if MIN plays,}
        \end{cases}
        \qquad q(s, a) = \sum_{s'} P(s' \mid s, a) \, v(s'),
    \]
    where $q(s, a)$ is the value of the chance node; a deterministic action has $P(\text{result}(s, a) \mid s, a) = 1$. An evaluation function must then be a positive linear transformation of the expected utility; preserving the order of states is not enough.

    \topic{Monte Carlo tree search}
    A \term{random playout} plays the game to its end with random moves; the fraction of playouts won evaluates a state. Each node $n$ keeps its visits $N(n)$ and the wins $Q(n, p)$ of each player $p$ over the playouts through $n$. A round does \term{selection}, \term{expansion}, \term{simulation} and \term{backpropagation}; selection picks the child $n'$ of $n$ that maximizes
    \[
        \frac{Q(n', p)}{N(n')} + c \sqrt{\frac{\log N(n)}{N(n')}},
    \]
    where $p$ is the player to move in $n$. The move played leads to the most visited child of the root.
\end{recap}

\titledquestion{Tic-tac-toe \aima{5}}

You are writing the engine of a program that plays Tic-Tac-Toe against human players. Tic-Tac-Toe is a game for two players, X and O, who take turns marking the cells of a $3 \times 3$ grid, X moving first. The player who succeeds in placing three of their marks in a straight line (horizontal, vertical or diagonal) wins the game. If neither of the players wins before the grid is full, it is a draw.

The program plays X, the MAX player, and its opponent O, the MIN player. We define $X_n$ as the number of rows, columns or diagonals with exactly $n$ X's and no O's. Similarly, $O_n$ is the number of rows, columns, or diagonals with just $n$ O's. A position $s$ is terminal if $X_3(s) \geq 1$, $O_3(s) \geq 1$ or if the grid is full. The utility function assigns $+1$, $-1$ or $0$ to such position, respectively. For non-terminal positions, we use an evaluation function defined as $\text{eval}(s) = 3 X_2(s) + X_1(s) - 3 O_2(s) - O_1(s)$.

\begin{parts}
    \part Define the search problem associated with the Tic-Tac-Toe game.

    \begin{solution}
        Let a state of the game be a matrix $s = (s_{ij}) \in \cbk{-1, 0, 1}^{3 \times 3}$, where the values $-1$, $0$ and $1$ respectively denote O, empty and X cells. X is MAX and O is MIN.
        \begin{description}
            \item[Initial state]
            \begin{equation*}
                s_1 = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \\ \end{pmatrix}
            \end{equation*}
            \item[Player function] X moves when there are as many X's as O's on the grid, and O otherwise,
            \begin{equation*}
                \text{player}(s) = \begin{cases}
                    \text{MAX} & \text{if } \sum_{i, j} s_{ij} = 0 \\
                    \text{MIN} & \text{otherwise.}
                \end{cases}
            \end{equation*}
            \item[Actions] An action is represented by the position $(i, j)$ of an empty cell in the grid. Then, the set of possible actions is $\text{actions}(s) = \cbk{(i, j): s_{ij} = 0}$.
            \item[Transition model] For an action $(i, j) \in \text{actions}(s)$, $\text{result}(s, (i, j)) = s'$ is the same as $s$ except that $s'_{ij} = 1$ if $\text{player}(s) = \text{MAX}$ and $-1$ otherwise.
            \item[Terminal test] $\text{terminal-test}(s)$ is true if $X_3(s) + O_3(s) \geq 1$ or $\prod_{i,j} s_{ij} \neq 0$, false otherwise.
            \item[Utility function] $\text{utility}(s, \text{MAX}) = 1$ if $X_3(s) \geq 1$, $-1$ if $O_3(s) \geq 1$ and $0$ otherwise.
        \end{description}
    \end{solution}

    \part Approximately how many possible game states of Tic-Tac-Toe are there?

    \begin{solution}
        Every cell being empty, X or O, there are at most $3^{3 \times 3} = \num{19683}$ states, unreachable ones included, and at most $\fact{9} = \num{362880}$ games, as many games end before the grid is full. The exact counts are \num{5478} reachable states and \num{255168} games.
    \end{solution}

        Let a state of the game be a tuple $s = (i, j, p) \in \cbk{1, 2, 3, 4}^2 \times \cbk{A, B}$, where $i$ and $j$ are the respective positions of $A$ and $B$ and $p$ is the player whose turn it is.

            \item[Player function] $\text{player}(s = (i, j, p)) = p$.
            \item[Actions] $\text{actions}(s) \subseteq \cbk{\text{left}, \text{right}}$.             \item[Transition model] For an available action $a \in \text{actions}(s)$,
            \begin{equation*}
                \text{result}(s = (i, j, p), a) = \begin{cases}
                    (\text{next}(i, j), j, B) & \text{if } p = A \text{ and } a = \text{right} \\
                    (\text{prev}(i, j), j, B) & \text{if } p = A \text{ and } a = \text{left} \\
                    (i, \text{next}(j, i), A) & \text{if } p = B \text{ and } a = \text{right} \\
                    (i, \text{prev}(j, i), A) & \text{if } p = B \text{ and } a = \text{left}

            \begin{align*}
                \text{next}(x, y) & = \begin{cases}
                    y + 1 & \text{if } x + 1 = y \\
                    x + 1 & \text{otherwise}

                \text{prev}(x, y) & = \begin{cases}
                    y - 1 & \text{if } x - 1 = y \\
                    x - 1 & \text{otherwise}
                \end{cases} .

            \item[Utility function]

                \text{utility}(s = (i, j, p), A) = \begin{cases}

    \part This race can be generalized to a tunnel of $n$ cells for any $n > 2$. Prove that $A$ wins if $n$ is even and loses if $n$ is odd.

    \begin{solution}

        \begin{itemize}
            \item \emph{$Q$ never jumps before the crossing.} $d = n - 2$ at the first turn of $A$ and $d = n - 3$ at the first turn of $B$, so $d$ is even at the first turn of $P$. Each move of $P$ decreases $d$ by 1 and each move of $Q$ changes it by $\pm 1$, so $d$ is even at every turn of $P$ and odd at every turn of $Q$. $Q$ therefore never faces an adjacent drone, the only case where it could jump.
            \item \emph{$P$ can always move forward.} It steps into a free cell if $d > 0$ and jumps over $Q$ if $d = 0$. The jump is impossible only if $Q$ stands on the goal of $P$, at the end of the tunnel, but $Q$ could only have moved there from the cell that $P$ now occupies, or by jumping over $P$, and neither is possible.
            \item \emph{The crossing.} Let $P$ jump on its $(m + 1)$-th move, after $m$ moves of one cell. Meanwhile, $Q$ made $m$ moves (if $P = A$) or $m + 1$ (if $P = B$), each bringing it at most one cell closer. The drones started $n - 2$ free cells apart, so $n - 2 \leq 2m + 1$, \ie{} $n - 3 - m \leq m$. After the jump, $P$ is $n - 3 - m$ cells from its goal and $Q$, to move, is $m + 1$ cells from its own.
            \item \emph{The race.} No drone stands between $Q$ and its goal any more, so $Q$ needs at least $m + 1$ moves, while $P$ reaches its goal in $n - 3 - m \leq m$ moves, before the $(m + 1)$-th move of $Q$. $Q$ can get in front of $P$ again only by jumping over it, two cells away from its own goal; $P$ then jumps over $Q$ in turn or, if $Q$ landed on the goal of $P$, steps back and jumps over $Q$ once $Q$, forced to move, has left that cell.
        \end{itemize}
        $P$ thus wins: $A$ wins if $n$ is even and $B$ if $n$ is odd.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

\titledquestion{Chess and transposition table \aima{5}}

Chess engines store the positions they have already evaluated in a transposition table, since many move orders lead to the same position. Suppose you write a chess engine that evaluates 16 million nodes per second (Deep Blue, which beat Garry Kasparov in 1997, evaluated about 200 million).

\begin{parts}
    \part Decide on a compact representation of a game state for storage in a transposition table.

    \begin{solution}
        There are 32 pieces, each on one of the 64 squares of the board, which takes \qty{6}{bits} ($2^6 = 64$); a captured piece can be put on the square of its own king, which is never captured. This makes $32 \times 6 = 192$ bits, \ie{} \qty{24}{B} per state. This is a lower bound: a complete entry also stores the side to move, the castling and en passant rights, the promoted pieces, which fixed piece slots cannot encode, and the value found by the search with its depth.
    \end{solution}

    \part About how many entries can you fit in a \qty{4}{\giga B} in-memory table?

    \begin{solution}
        We can store roughly $\num{4e9} \times \frac{1}{24} \approx \num{1.7e8}$ states in the table.
    \end{solution}

    \part Will that be enough for the three minutes of search allocated for one move?

    \begin{solution}
        It is not enough to store all $\num{16e6} \times 3 \times 60 = \num{2.88e9}$ evaluated nodes, about 17 times more.
    \end{solution}

    \part How many table lookups can you do in the time it would take to do one evaluation? Suppose that you have a \qty{3.2}{\giga\hertz} machine and that it takes 20 operations to do one lookup on the transposition table.

    \begin{solution}
        We can perform
        \begin{equation*}
            \frac{\num{3.2e9}}{\num{16e6}} \times \frac{1}{20} = 10
        \end{equation*}
        table lookups in the same amount of time as a single evaluation. A lookup thus costs a tenth of an evaluation, and a hit saves at least that evaluation: the table pays off as soon as more than one lookup in ten finds its state.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}

    \part Which child does UCB1 select with $c = 0$? With $c = 1$? Use the natural logarithm.

    \begin{solution}
        The exploitation terms $Q(n', p) / N(n')$ are $0.667$, $0.5$ and $0.5$, and the exploration terms $\sqrt{\log N(n) / N(n')}$ are $0.500$, $0.707$ and $1.224$, with $\log 20 = 3.00$. With $c = 0$, UCB1 selects $n'_1$, the best win rate. With $c = 1$, the scores are $1.166$, $1.207$ and $1.724$, and UCB1 selects $n'_3$, the least visited child.
    \end{solution}

    \part For which values of $c$ does UCB1 select $n'_1$?

    \begin{solution}
        $n'_1$ beats $n'_3$ if $0.667 + 0.500 c \geq 0.5 + 1.224 c$, \ie{} $c \leq 0.23$, and beats $n'_2$ if $0.667 + 0.500 c \geq 0.5 + 0.707 c$, \ie{} $c \leq 0.81$. UCB1 thus selects $n'_1$ for $c \leq 0.23$: the smaller $c$, the more UCB1 exploits the best win rate so far, and the larger $c$, the more it explores the children visited least.
    \end{solution}
\end{parts}
